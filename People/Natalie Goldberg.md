# Natalie Goldberg

![[Natalie Goldberg — hero.png]]

Natalie Goldberg (1948–) studied with [[Shunryu Suzuki]]'s Zen lineage for years before she figured out what to do with it: not sit longer, but write the same way she sat. *Writing Down the Bones* (1986) is the result — a small book that turned the blank page into a meditation cushion. Her method has one rule and it never gets more complicated than this: set a timer, keep the hand moving, don't stop to fix anything until the timer ends.

## Keep the hand moving

Goldberg's writing practice is timed and rule-bound on purpose: ten minutes, pen never lifts, no crossing out, no reaching for a better word. The constraint isn't there to produce good sentences — it's there to shut off the editor long enough for something truthful to get out. She calls what comes out **first thoughts**: the sentence before the inner critic has had time to soften it. First thoughts are usually rougher than a revised draft and truer than one. You don't judge them mid-flow; you write them down and let a later pass decide what stays.

That later pass is **composting** — her word for the unglamorous fact that raw material needs time underground before it's usable. A freewrite isn't the finished piece; it's mulch. Some of it becomes soil for something else weeks later. This maps onto studio work almost without translation: a first take is a first thought, a rough mix left to sit overnight is compost, and the discipline of not touching either too soon is the same discipline in both rooms.

## Monkey mind, met without a fight

The distraction that shows up mid-practice — the grocery list, the old argument replaying, the voice saying *this is bad* — Goldberg calls monkey mind, and her instruction is not to defeat it. Notice it, let it chatter, keep writing anyway. This is the same move [[Shunryu Suzuki|Suzuki]] teaches with the breath: you don't win against distraction, you return to the page the way you return to the count of one. Writing practice, for Goldberg, *is* zen practice wearing a different shirt — the sitting cushion swapped for a notebook, the breath swapped for a pen that doesn't stop.

## Process over product

> "This is your life. You are responsible for it. You will not live forever. Don't wait."

Goldberg's urgency isn't about output — it's about showing up honestly, today, to the practice itself, which is exactly the stance [[Quality Manifesto]] takes when it separates the work from what it's measured by: the timer session isn't justified by what it produces later, it's justified by having happened at all. And when all four pillars are actually firing in a session — a real page being made, a timer as the tool, monkey mind met as philosophy, the daily repetition as practice — that's a small [[Hilaritas Generator]] running on nothing but a pen and a clock.

> "Write what disturbs you, what you fear, what you have not been willing to speak about. Be willing to be split open."

That's the other half of the practice: once the editor is off, aim the hand at what actually matters, not what's safe to say.

## Recommended path

- **Start here:** *Writing Down the Bones* (1986) — short chapters, each one a single instruction; read a few pages, then write for ten minutes before reading more.
- **Next:** *Wild Mind* (1990) — the practice extended past the page into a whole way of paying attention.
- **Later:** *The True Secret of Writing* (2013) — Goldberg after decades of teaching, closing the loop back to formal sitting practice.
