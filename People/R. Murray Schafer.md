# R. Murray Schafer

![[R. Murray Schafer — hero.png]]

R. Murray Schafer (1933–2021) — Canadian composer, teacher, founder of the World Soundscape Project — made one argument his whole career: the world is already a composition, and most of us have stopped listening to it. He gives the palace its outdoor ear. Where the studio pillars teach you to build a sound from nothing, Schafer teaches you to notice the sound that was already there before you touched a fader.

## The soundscape

His term, and it does real work: **soundscape** treats an acoustic environment the way you'd treat a piece of music — as a composition worth analyzing, not just ambient background.

> "The soundscape is any acoustic field of study. We may speak of a musical composition as a soundscape, or a radio program as a soundscape, or an acoustic environment as a soundscape."

A soundscape has a structure. **Keynote sounds** are the ones that define a place without anyone noticing them — traffic hum, ocean, wind through a particular kind of tree — the tonal center everything else sits against. **Signals** are the sounds foregrounded on purpose — a bell, a horn, a voice raised to be heard. Schafer also ranked environments **hi-fi** (rural, low background noise, a dropped pin travels — every sound distinct against a quiet floor) against **lo-fi** (urban, so much competing sound that signals get buried and nothing carries). A hi-fi soundscape lets you hear detail and distance; a lo-fi one flattens everything into noise. That's the same distinction a mix engineer makes between a track with headroom and one crushed flat — Schafer just made it about a city instead of a channel strip.

> "Noise is any sound we have learned to ignore."

## Schizophonia — the split that makes dub possible

Schafer's least comfortable idea, and the one that lands hardest here: **schizophonia**, the split between a sound and its original source. A voice recorded is no longer tied to the mouth that made it — it can be played anywhere, any number of times, forever separated from the moment it happened. He coined the word with some alarm; recording technology, to him, was a wound in the natural order of sound-and-source.

But a wound is exactly where [[Dub Lineage]] starts working. Dub is what happens once you accept the split instead of mourning it — once the voice is free of the throat, you can loop it, echo it, strip the riddim down to its skeleton and rebuild the track as its own version. Schafer names the condition; dub is one answer to it, and not the only possible one. Every sample, every field recording pulled into a device Loudon builds, every looped phrase in [[Retrospective Delay]] is a schizophonic act — a sound doing something apart from its origin. Naming the split is what lets you use it on purpose instead of by accident.

## Ear cleaning

Schafer's practice, not just his theory: **ear cleaning**, listening exercises built to undo the ignoring that noise trains into you. Go outside. Don't record — just listen. Notice the keynote, then the signals riding on top of it, then whatever you'd normally filter out as noise. Ten minutes, no output, no product. This is a soundwalk, and it is the same move [[Hilaritas Generator]] makes when it insists a practice can be the whole point rather than a step toward one — the listening itself increases what you're capable of hearing next time.

He shares this ground with [[Pauline Oliveros]], whose Deep Listening is the same discipline turned inward — training attention to hold a whole field of sound rather than one spotlighted part. Schafer sends you outdoors to find the score already playing; Oliveros trains the instrument doing the hearing. And he shares it with [[John Cage]], who proved with *4'33"* that there's no such thing as a silent room, only one you'd stopped attending to. Cage staged that discovery in a concert hall; Schafer just says: you didn't need the hall. Every room, every street, every field is already doing it.

## Production as curation, not creation from nothing

The synthesis Schafer hands the [[FOUR PILLARS]]: if the world is already composing, then recording and production are acts of *selection and framing*, not summoning something from silence. You choose what to keep, what to foreground as signal, what to let sit as keynote. That reframes field recording as composition rather than raw material waiting to become one — the choosing already is the compositional act.

## Recommended path

- **Read:** *The Soundscape: Our Sonic Environment and the Tuning of the World* (1977) — best read while doing soundwalks, so the ideas land on real air rather than the page.
- **Practice:** ear cleaning, daily if possible — ten minutes outside, no recording, just noticing keynote, signal, and what you'd normally call noise.
