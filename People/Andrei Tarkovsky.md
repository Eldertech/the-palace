# Andrei Tarkovsky

![[Andrei Tarkovsky — hero.png]]

Andrei Tarkovsky (1932–1986) made films so slow that watching one is itself a small discipline. He gives the palace its clearest statement that **time is a material** — not the container a story happens in, but the thing you shape directly, the way a sculptor shapes marble.

## Sculpting in time

> "Just as a sculptor takes a lump of marble, and inwardly conscious of the features of his finished piece, removes everything that is not part of it — so the film-maker, from a 'lump of time' made up of an enormous, solid cluster of living facts, cuts off and discards whatever he does not need."

A film isn't moving pictures. It's shaped duration. His signature tool is the long take — some shots in *Stalker* (1979) run four or five minutes unbroken, against a modern average of a few seconds. The long take forces you to wait, and the waiting is not a cost you pay to get to the meaning — it *is* the meaning. Cut fast and you deliver information. Hold the shot and you deliver an experience of duration itself. Loudon's music carries the same choice: a track arranged for a three-minute hook and a track that lets a pattern breathe for fifteen are shaping time two different ways, and neither is wrong — but you should know which one you're doing. That's the plain version of [[FOUR PILLARS]]' practice pillar: rhythm and pacing aren't decoration on top of the work, they're a decision about what the work *is*.

Tarkovsky's films also move like memory rather than plot — associative, non-linear, an image calling up another image instead of A causing B causing C. "Time is a state," he wrote, "the flame in which there lives the salamander of the human soul." Structure by feeling instead of formula is the same subtraction as the long take: strip out what a tidy narrative would insist on, and what's left is closer to how a mind actually holds an experience.

## Patience as a shared practice

This is where Tarkovsky stops being a filmmaker the palace admires from a distance and starts being one voice in a conversation. [[James Turrell]] asks you to sit in a Skyspace for thirty minutes while the sky changes so slowly you can't catch it changing — same wager as the long take, in light instead of celluloid: perception needs unbroken duration to do its work. [[Pauline Oliveros]] asks for the same patience turned toward sound — ten minutes of deep listening reveal a sonic field that a glance would miss entirely. Three different materials — image, light, sound — trained on one discovery: rushing doesn't just make things worse, it makes certain kinds of depth structurally impossible to reach. You cannot get there faster. The three of them are triangulating the same fact from different studios.

Tarkovsky's stillness also asks something of the *shape* of the image, not just its duration, and that's the seam [[Comic and Cinema — Two Ways of Seeing]] names directly: a comic panel compresses time into a still the reader must animate themselves; a long take dilates time into duration the viewer simply undergoes. Tarkovsky is the cinematic pole pushed to its limit — maximum dilation, minimum cutting, the camera trusting the moment enough to just let it run.

## Against the metric

> Not entertainment. Not information. Revelation.

Tarkovsky treated filmmaking as something closer to a spiritual practice than a product built to hold attention, and that's the direct ancestor of the [[Quality Manifesto]]'s refusal of engagement metrics. Box office and hooks-in-the-first-three-seconds are "their quality" — optimized for reach. Tarkovsky's quality was depth, and depth has never been measurable by the same yardstick as attention. The manifesto's "depth over reach" isn't a new idea dressed in music-industry language; it's Tarkovsky's bet, restated for a livestream instead of a 35mm reel.

None of this means everything should be slow. A three-second cut is correct for urgency the way a five-minute take is correct for contemplation — the point is to choose the pace on purpose, in service of what the piece is actually trying to give someone, instead of defaulting to fast because fast is what the platform rewards.

## Recommended path

- **Read:** *Sculpting in Time* (1986) — his own film philosophy, read slowly, the way he'd want it read.
- **Watch in order:** *Andrei Rublev* (1966, accessible entry point) → *Solaris* (1972, a grief film disguised as science fiction) → *Stalker* (1979, the long take pushed furthest) → *Mirror* (1975, save for last — non-linear, autobiographical, the hardest one to hold).
- **Watch it whole**, uninterrupted, no second screen. If the first thirty minutes feel slow, stay with that feeling rather than reaching for a skip button — it's the material doing its work on you.
