# Epictetus — speech

Ground-truth voice evidence. The `## Voice` note in [[Epictetus]] is a compile *from this file*.

## Sources & their limits (READ THIS — the double veil)

**Epictetus wrote nothing.** No autograph, no recording. What survives is a **triple remove**: (1) his **spoken** classroom lectures/dialogues, (2) transcribed/composed by his student **Arrian** into Greek prose (*Discourses* c. 108 CE; the terser *Enchiridion* digest), (3) carried through **centuries of translation** (Carter 1758 → Hard 2014). Every quote below is at least three removes from his mouth.

- **But the spoken register is credible.** Scholars (SEP) find Arrian preserves common *koinē* Greek with a "brusque, elliptical manner" — reading as real transcription, not literary invention. The *Discourses* keep the **diatribe** form: blunt second-person "you," an imagined objector, rhetorical questions, homely examples (the bath, lost property, a dead child, the actor), a rhetorical/sometimes mocking classroom voice.
- **The *Enchiridion*** is the later, terser handbook distillation — punchier, aphoristic, less argumentative texture.
- **Translation is a constant veil** — most famous English phrasings trace to Carter (1758).
- **Flag:** "It's not what happens to you, but how you react to it that matters" is a **modern paraphrase**, not a primary line — prefer the sourced Handbook-5 wording (#2). Discourses #9 (below) location was unpinned in research — re-verify before hard-citing.

## The register, in one breath

Blunt, tough-love, in a hurry; second-person "you," imagined objector, rhetorical questions, homely examples; sometimes mocking or taunting; drives the point into the gut, not the notebook.

## Excerpts (verbatim, tagged)

1. "Of things some are in our power, and others are not. In our power are opinion, movement towards a thing, desire, aversion… and, in a word, whatever are our own acts: not in our power are the body, property, reputation, offices… and, in a word, whatever are not our own acts."
   — *Enchiridion* 1. **[handbook — terse digest]** — *the whole system's opening.*

2. "Men are disturbed, not by things, but by the principles and notions which they form concerning things."
   — *Enchiridion* 5 (Carter, 1758). **[handbook]** — *the verified form; use this, not the "how you react" paraphrase.*

3. "Don't demand that things happen as you wish, but wish that they happen as they do happen, and you will go on well."
   — *Enchiridion* 8. **[handbook]**

4. "If you are going to bathe, put before your mind what happens in the bath — water pouring over some, others being jostled, some reviling, others stealing… 'I did not want only to bathe, but to keep my will in harmony with nature.'"
   — *Enchiridion* 4. **[handbook — but the homely bath example is the spoken diatribe register underneath]**

5. "Never say of anything, 'I have lost it'; but, 'I have returned it.' Is your child dead? It is returned. Is your wife dead? She is returned."
   — *Enchiridion* 11. **[handbook]** — *the passage most likely to land as troubling to a grieving reader; keep it cold. (Blindspot §c.)*

6. "Remember that you are an actor in a drama, of such a kind as the author pleases to make it… act well the character assigned you; to choose it is another's."
   — *Enchiridion* 17. **[handbook]**

7. "Slave [yourself], will you not bear with your own brother, who has Zeus for his progenitor… that they are kinsmen, that they are brethren by nature, that they are the offspring of Zeus?"
   — *Discourses* I.13. **[edited print · Arrian's transcription (spoken origin), diatribe — direct rebuke of a slave-owner]** — *the moral-seriousness exception; note "Slave!" is still the insult doing the work.*

8. "But I have purchased them, and they have not purchased me… it is towards these wretched laws of dead men?"
   — *Discourses* I.13. **[edited print · Arrian's transcription]** — *dismissing Roman slave law.*

9. "Show me one who is sick and yet happy, in peril and yet happy, dying and yet happy, in exile and happy, in disgrace and happy. Show him me. By the gods I would fain see a Stoic."
   — *Discourses* II.19. **[edited print · Arrian's transcription, diatribe — the taunting/testing tone; his hard test of what Stoic joy claims]**
