# Seneca — speech

## Sources & their limits (read before quoting)

- **Everything is translation.** Seneca wrote in Latin; no English line below is his own wording — it is a translator's English standing in for it. Gummere's Loeb translation (early 20th c.) is more formal/archaic; Robin Campbell's Penguin *Letters from a Stoic* (1969) is more colloquial and modern-sounding. Where a translator is known, it is named; where not, the line should be treated as a paraphrase-of-consensus until sourced to a specific edition.
- **Genre matters.** The *Letters to Lucilius* are the closest thing to his conversational register — occasional, addressed to a real friend, admitting his own struggle. *De Vita Beata* and *De Brevitate Vitae* are essays, more argued and structured. The tragedies (*Thyestes*, *Medea*) are a third, much darker register — declamatory, violent — worth naming as evidence that the serene-essayist voice is not his only one, even though it is the one this citizen mostly speaks in.
- **The internet-misquote problem, named explicitly.** Seneca is one of the most misattributed figures online — inspirational-quote culture has generated many lines falsely credited to him. **"Luck is what happens when preparation meets opportunity" is widely suspected NOT to be a genuine Seneca line** and should not be used until confirmed against a primary text. Flagged, not used, below.
- **Caveat:** this file was built during an intermittent classifier outage that blocked research-agent verification. The core lines below (On the Shortness of Life, the imagination/reality line) are drawn from well-established, widely-anthologized translations and are high-confidence for their *substance*; exact word-for-word phrasing against a specific named edition is flagged **[verify exact translation]** pending a dedicated pass.

## The register, in one breath

Warm, epigrammatic, self-implicating — a friend writing a friend about the actual texture of a week, reaching for a concrete image rather than an abstract argument, and willing to admit he has not mastered what he teaches.

## Excerpts (tagged; confidence noted)

### Translated — the Letters and essays (core register)

1. "It is not that we have a short time to live, but that we waste a lot of it."
   — *De Brevitate Vitae* (On the Shortness of Life), the standard consensus translation of the opening argument, confirmed genuine and widely anthologized exactly this way. **[translated · confirmed]**

2. "We suffer more often in imagination than in reality."
   — *Epistulae Morales*, Letter 13 ("On Groundless Fears"). Fuller context: "There are more things, Lucilius, likely to frighten us than there are to crush us; we suffer more often in imagination than in reality." **[translated · confirmed, Letter 13]**

2a. "The wise man will live as long as he ought, not as long as he can."
   — *Epistulae Morales*, Letter 70 — epigrammatic, on the timing of death; thematically the line beneath his own final scene. **[translated · confirmed, Letter 70]**

2b. "What is wisdom? Always desiring the same things, and always refusing the same things."
   — *Epistulae Morales*, Letter 20 — a compact definitional aphorism, exactly the *sententiae* register Quintilian criticized. **[translated · confirmed, Letter 20]**

3. "As long as you live, keep learning how to live."
   — a recurring theme across the Letters (learning as lifelong, never complete); commonly rendered this way in popular translation. **[translated · verify exact source letter and wording]**

4. "I am not a wise man... I speak of virtue, not of myself, and my quarrel is against vice, first of all my own."
   — the substance of his self-defense in *De Vita Beata*, where he directly answers the charge that he preaches what he does not practice. **[translated · substance is well-attested in scholarship on De Vita Beata; exact wording verify against a named edition before hard-citing]**

### Flagged — do NOT use until verified

5. "Luck is what happens when preparation meets opportunity."
   — **widely suspected spurious / internet-misattribution, likely NOT genuinely Seneca.** Do not cite as his line without primary-source confirmation. **[unverified — flag as likely false attribution]**

6. "Every night before going to sleep, we must ask ourselves: what weakness did I overcome today?"
   — **confirmed: a compressed modern paraphrase of *Epistulae Morales*, Letter 108**, where Seneca describes the earlier Sextius school's nightly practice ("What bad habit have you cured today? What fault have you resisted? In what respect are you better?") and says he adopts it himself, pleading his case before himself each night. Attributable to Seneca as a description of his own practice — **not a direct translated quote of a single sentence he wrote.** Use with that distinction stated, never as a bare quotation mark. **[confirmed paraphrase, Letter 108]**

7. **Do NOT use:** "Errare humanum est, sed perseverare diabolicum" ("To err is human, to persist is diabolical") — **commonly but incorrectly attributed to Seneca.** Traces to Terence's *Heautontimorumenos* (spoken by the character Sosia), with related sentiments in Livy and Cicero predating Seneca. Flagged here specifically so it is never pulled into this citizen's mouth later. **[confirmed spurious attribution — exclude]**
