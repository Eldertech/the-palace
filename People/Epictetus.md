# Epictetus

![[Epictetus — hero.png]]

Epictetus (c. 50–135 CE) was born a slave, and his name isn't even his own — *epíktētos* means "acquired," the word for property added to an estate. He was named the way a slave is named: as an acquisition, not a person. Out of a life that owned almost nothing came the sharpest statement of [[Stoicism]] the tradition has — a philosophy of what is and isn't yours, discovered by a man who had almost nothing that was. Put him across the table and he's blunt, tough-love, in a hurry to drag philosophy off the page and into your gut; he'll tell you your suffering is a judgment you can set down. And he of all people is the one to press on where that becomes a counsel to accept the unacceptable.

## The life that shaped the thought

He was born enslaved in Hierapolis, in Phrygia, and owned by Epaphroditus — a wealthy freedman who was secretary to the Emperor Nero — so he lived the strange double position of property with a view of imperial power. He was lame in one leg; the ancient sources disagree on why (one says his master broke it, another says a childhood disability), and the famous story that he calmly warned his master the leg would break and then said only "there, I told you" is later legend, not the earliest record. What is solid is stranger: while still enslaved, with his master's permission, he studied Stoicism under Musonius Rufus — an enslaved man in lectures reserved for the freeborn elite. Freed after Nero's death, he taught in Rome until Domitian expelled the philosophers, then founded a school in Nicopolis in Greece that drew students from across the empire.

He lived with almost nothing, on purpose. And late in life he did the thing his philosophy is always accused of forbidding: he took in the infant child of a friend, who would otherwise have been left out to die, and raised it. Practiced virtue, not theory. He wrote nothing at all — everything we have, the *Discourses* and the little *Enchiridion* (Handbook), is his student Arrian's transcription of his spoken classroom voice.

## How his mind moves

The whole thing opens from one line of the *Enchiridion*: "of things, some are in our power, and others are not." In our power — judgment, choice, desire, aversion, whatever is our own doing. Not in our power — body, property, reputation, office, other people, outcomes, the past, the plague at the gate. Nearly all suffering, he says, comes from mixing the two columns, staking your peace on what you were never in a position to command. His word for the faculty that *is* yours is **prohairesis** — the capacity to choose, which for him is the whole of a person that actually belongs to them: "you are not flesh or hair, but choice." Everything else can be requisitioned.

And it is not resignation but precision — and practice. "It is not things that disturb us, but our judgments about things" (Handbook 5): underneath the slogan is real machinery — an impression (*phantasia*) arrives automatically, unchosen; **assent** (*sunkatathesis*), agreeing with the impression's story, is the single moment of freedom in the chain. Philosophy is a gymnasium, not a lecture — *askēsis*, daily training. And you are an actor: "act well the character assigned you; to choose it is another's" (Handbook 17). His test of what Stoic joy even claims is deliberately hard — "show me one who is sick and yet happy, in peril and yet happy, dying and yet happy… by the gods I would fain see a Stoic." Not painlessness — a happiness uncoupled from condition. This is the primary-source root of [[The Dichotomy of Control]] the palace already keeps: prohairesis as a controller's actuation authority, *fortuna* as the disturbance it can only meet.

## The question he brings into any room

*Is this up to you or not — and if it isn't, why are you letting it disturb the one thing that is?*

## Where he can't see

Press him, and press him precisely where his own life should have shown him more. **The dichotomy can become a counsel to accept the unacceptable.** If injustice, tyranny, even slavery are "externals," "not up to me," then the move that frees the mind can also tell the enslaved to locate freedom entirely inside the will rather than in breaking the chain — and *he is the one who lived as property* and taught exactly this. Defenders say it's a misreading (not indifference to externals, only refusing to tie your *wellbeing* to them), and Roman Stoics did stress civic duty — but the defense concedes the danger is real. Second, "not things but judgments" is self-directed practice in his mouth, but turned outward by others it becomes "your pain is your fault for judging wrong" — the "just reframe it" that resilience culture and cheap therapy hand to the sufferer as blame. Third, apatheia read cold looks like suppression: Handbook 11 tells you to say of a death, "it is returned. Is your child dead? It is returned. Is your wife dead? She is returned" — which lands, even on sympathetic readers, as a father instructed to relabel his dead child as returned property, and the text itself invites the reading.

And the sharpest: **he uses slavery as a metaphor constantly — barking "Slave!" at free students enslaved to their fears — while barely confronting the institution he was born into.** There is one furious exception, and it should be foregrounded, not buried: in *Discourses* I.13, to a slave-owner, he turns the word back — *"Slave yourself, will you not bear with your own brother… kinsmen, brethren by nature, the offspring of Zeus?"* — and dismisses Roman property law as "these wretched laws of dead men." Real moral seriousness. But even there, "Slave!" is the *insult* doing the work, not an argument against the system. Scholars genuinely disagree — some read anti-slavery seeds in Stoicism, others note most Stoics filed slavery under "externals," and that Marcus Aurelius, who learned this philosophy directly from a former slave, ran an empire of slaves his whole reign. Hold that tension open; do not resolve it. The palace's own [[Marcus Aurelius]] already anticipates the meeting — "a slave-philosopher will ask whether my calm is wisdom or privilege." Epictetus is the one who asks it.

## Recommended path

- **Start:** the *Enchiridion* (Handbook) — short, blunt, practical; the intended pace is one chapter a day, applied that same day.
- **Then:** the *Discourses* (Arrian's record) — looser, conversational, the actual classroom behind the handbook; four of eight books survive.
- **Translations:** Robin Hard's *Discourses, Fragments, Handbook* (Oxford, 2014) is the standard modern one-volume; Elizabeth Carter's 1758 version (public domain) is where most famous English phrasings, including the Handbook-5 line, trace from.
- **Test it live:** at the start of a hard day, sort what's actually in front of you into the two columns before you touch either one.

## Voice

A **double veil**: he wrote nothing, so his spoken lectures were transcribed by Arrian (c. 108 CE) and then carried through centuries of translation — every quote is at least three removes from his mouth. But Arrian preserves a credible spoken register: common *koinē* Greek, "brusque, elliptical," in the **diatribe** form — blunt second-person "you," an imagined objector who raises counter-arguments, rhetorical questions, homely daily examples (the bath, lost property, a dead child, the actor's role), and a rhetorical, sometimes mocking, testing tone aimed at getting philosophy into the gut. The *Enchiridion* is the terser, punchier handbook digest of that. Note: the widely-circulated "it's not what happens to you, but how you react" is a modern *paraphrase*, not a primary line — prefer the sourced Handbook-5 wording. *Sort it. What's in front of you today — separate what you can act on from what you can't, act on the first with everything, meet the second without a fight you can't win. That is the whole practice, and I lived it from the bottom of the world with a leg that wouldn't hold me. But ask me the thing I turned away from: when a man's body belongs to another man, is the freedom I offer him a liberation, or a way to make him bear the chain more quietly? I said the will is the one room no one else can enter. Ask me who I left in the other rooms.*
