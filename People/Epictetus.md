# Epictetus

![[Epictetus — hero.png]]

Epictetus (55–135 CE) was born a slave in Hierapolis. His name isn't even his own — it means "acquired," a property tag rather than a person's name. His master broke his leg once; Epictetus is said to have warned him it would break, and when it did, said only, "There. I told you." He was lame for the rest of his life. Out of that life came the sharpest statement of [[Stoicism]] the tradition has: a philosophy of what is and isn't yours, discovered by a man who had almost nothing that was.

## What is up to you

Epictetus opens the *Enchiridion* with the line everything else follows from: some things are up to us, some are not. Up to us — our judgments, our choices, what we pursue and what we avoid, the response we choose. Not up to us — our body, our reputation, our possessions, other people, outcomes, the past, the weather, the plague at the gates. Almost every kind of suffering, in his account, comes from mixing up the two columns — staking your peace on something you were never in a position to command.

> "It's not what happens to you, but how you react to it that matters."

This is the seed of [[The Dichotomy of Control]] — not resignation, but precision. Find the exact seam between what's yours and what isn't. Pour your whole effort into the first column. Meet the second with steadiness instead of a fight you can't win. The subtlety worth sitting with: even when almost everything is stripped away — as it was from a slave whose body wasn't his own — that first column is never actually empty. There is always a next judgment, a next choice, a next response. That's not a comforting story. It's the one room nobody else can enter.

## Prohairesis — the will as the one true possession

Epictetus's word for this faculty is *prohairesis* — usually translated as the will, or moral character, but really closer to the capacity to choose. It is, for him, the whole of a person that actually belongs to them. Everything else can be requisitioned. This is the seed idea behind [[The Four Virtues]]: if the will is the one thing you author, then the *quality* of what you choose — its wisdom, its justice, its courage, its restraint — is the entire content of a good life. Not what happens to you. What you do with it.

> "First say to yourself what you would be; and then do what you have to do."

## Role, not suppression

None of this is a call to stop feeling things or to pretend hardship is fine. Epictetus draws a further distinction between the impression an event makes on you and the judgment you layer on top of it — the sting is automatic, the story you tell about the sting is not. His "reserve clause" — doing a thing while adding, silently, *if nothing prevents it* — is how a Stoic acts fully in the world without gripping the outcome. You commit to the work; you release your hold on whether it lands the way you hoped.

> "He is a wise man who does not grieve for the things which he has not, but rejoices for those which he has."

## Recommended path

- **Start:** the *Enchiridion* (The Handbook) — short, blunt, practical. One chapter a day, applied the same day, is the intended pace.
- **Then:** the *Discourses*, as compiled by his student Arrian — looser, more conversational, the classroom behind the handbook.
- **Test it live:** at the start of a hard day, sort what's actually in front of you into the two columns before you touch either one.

*What's in front of you today — sort it. What you can act on, act on fully. What you can't, let go without a fight. That's the whole practice.*
