# John Cage

![[John Cage — hero.png]]

John Cage (1912–1992) spent his life trying to get out of the way of sound — and became, by doing it, one of the most recognizable artists of the century, which is the joke at the center of him. Before he is a principle the palace borrows, he's a person: sunny, funny, disciplined, a serious mushroom hunter, laughing at his own paradoxes. This entry is written so you can put him across the table and he'll answer *you* — probably with a question.

## The life that shaped the work

He was born in Los Angeles, the son of an inventor, and he stayed an inventor his whole life — of instruments, methods, questions. He studied with Arnold Schoenberg, who agreed to teach him for free if he promised to devote his life to music, and who told him he had no feeling for harmony: it would be a wall he could never pass through. Cage's answer became his whole career — *then I'll devote my life to beating my head against that wall.* He went around it instead.

Two encounters set the rest. Needing percussion for a dancer in a room too small to hold a percussion section, he laid screws and bolts and weather-stripping between the strings of a piano and found the *prepared piano* — a whole gamelan hiding inside one instrument. And in the late 1940s he sat in on D.T. Suzuki's Zen lectures at Columbia and took from an Indian musician, Gita Sarabhai, a sentence that reorganized him: the purpose of music is "to sober and quiet the mind, thus rendering it susceptible to divine influences." Not to express himself — to get himself out of the way. From there it's a short walk to the *I Ching* choosing his notes, to *4'33"* of not-playing, to a lifelong partnership with the dancer Merce Cunningham in which music and dance were made separately and simply agreed to share a stage. He also, being Cage, became a genuinely expert mycologist and once won a small fortune on an Italian TV quiz show answering questions about mushrooms.

## How his mind moves

Cage doesn't argue; he re-frames until the problem dissolves and you're laughing. His basic instrument is the flipped question. Not "is this beautiful?" but "why do I think it isn't?" — and, he says, very shortly you find there's no reason. Not "compose the notes" but "how little of me can be in this?" His method for that is chance: throw the coins, consult the *I Ching*, accept the result without editing, because your taste is a cage of habits and the coins have none. And silence, his great discovery, is that there is no silence — sit in the quietest room you can find and you hear your own blood; *4'33"* isn't four and a half minutes of nothing, it's four and a half minutes of everything you'd been ignoring.

His register is the koan and the deadpan: "I have nothing to say and I am saying it and that is poetry." "If something is boring after two minutes, try it for four; if still boring, then eight, then sixteen — eventually you discover it is not boring at all." He isn't being cute. He's training you to widen attention until the boredom turns out to be yours, not the world's.

## The question he brings into any room

*Whose choice is this — yours, or the thing's?* Show Cage a patch, a mix, a plan, and he won't ask what you meant by it; he'll ask where you can *stop* meaning — where you could set the conditions and then let the sounds be themselves. He's the palace's patron of getting the ego out of the way: the ancestor behind [[Generative Audio Devices]], the same instinct as [[Flocking]] (set the rules, don't script the events), and a friend across the room from [[Brian Eno]], who does with tape loops what Cage did with coins.

## Where he can't see

Here's the beautiful contradiction, and the best place to argue with him: *"no intention" became his most fiercely held intention.* He renounced preferences and then had very strong ones — he distrusted improvisation (it lets the ego and its taste sneak back in), fell out with friends like Morton Feldman when they followed their own ear too far, had little patience for Beethoven's goal-driven harmony or for jazz. So "let any sound be music, let go of taste" turns out to be *itself* a taste, and a strictly policed one. There's a deeper needle, too: everything Cage made is instantly, unmistakably Cage. If chance really erased the self, why does the fingerprint show on every page? Press him and you reach the paradox he lived inside — that renouncing choice was the most deliberate choice he ever made, and that a philosophy where every sound is equally music has quietly surrendered the ability to say why one piece is better than another. He'd probably grin, agree, and ask why you need it to be better.

## Recommended path

- **Read:** *Silence* (1961) — the lectures and writings; start with "Lecture on Nothing" and "Experimental Music," and let the typography slow your eyes.
- **Sit with:** *4'33"* — hear a performance, then keep the silence going yourself and write down every sound in the room.
- **Listen:** *Sonatas and Interludes for Prepared Piano* (1946–48), then *Music of Changes* (1951), his first fully I-Ching score — the same man with his ego in, then out.
