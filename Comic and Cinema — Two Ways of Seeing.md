# Comic and Cinema — Two Ways of Seeing

<!-- CLAUDE → LOUDON: hero + icon pending — run the Hero and Avatar Maker distiller. Apt idiom: a split image, one half stark ink panel (comic), one half soft photographic light (cinema), the seam down the middle — no text. -->
![[Comic and Cinema — Two Ways of Seeing — hero.png]]

Two ways of telling a story in pictures, pulling in opposite directions. Comics and film are not one craft with different budgets — they ask opposite things of the picture and opposite things of the viewer.

**The comic way — compress and abstract.** A comic gives you still pictures with gaps between them, and *you* supply the motion, the time, the in-between. McCloud calls that filling-in **closure**, and the blank gutter between panels is where it happens. The drawing gets *simpler* on purpose — a few strong lines, the inessential stripped — because an abstract face is easier to step inside than a photograph of a stranger. Time is drawn as space: a wider panel simply lasts longer. The reader does a lot of the work, and that participation is the point.

**The cinematic way — dilate and embody.** Film hands you continuous motion and real duration. The camera moves so you don't have to imagine the move; a second on screen *is* a second; the photographic image is specific — this face, this light, this room. Where the comic abstracts so you can project yourself in, cinema embodies so you can witness. The viewer does less filling-in and more being-carried.

**The seam.** [[BLUELINE]] is built across this divide: it **storyboards in the comic way** (compressed, abstract, a panel that asks the reader to finish it) and then **renders toward the cinematic** (it moves, it embodies). That join is exactly where [[Adopt the Craft, Author the Seam]] tells us to spend our novelty — the transduction between the two ways of seeing. [[Frame Designer]] works the same hinge: it stages a frame in comics and pushes it toward film.

**Why it lives in the palace.** This is a teaching tool as much as a making tool. A lot of what we build has to decide, shot by shot: do I draw one still that makes the learner do the work (compress), or let it move and carry them (dilate)? Naming the two ways of seeing makes that a *choice* instead of a habit — and storytelling that teaches well usually knows which one it is using. It [[deepens]] [[Graphic Storytelling]] (the comic way is that craft's native tongue) and couples to [[The Reader's Context Window]] — the gap only works if you know what the reader can be trusted to fill.
