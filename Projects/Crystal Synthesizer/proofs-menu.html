What the numbers say — and where the hypothesis bends The home entry ladders its predictions by symmetry class: cubic is brightest and densest, triclinic is the most scattered. Measured spectral centroid does not follow that ladder. Ruby (trigonal, seven modes) is the darkest sound in the whole palette at 130 Hz — darker than cubic diamond — because all seven of its modes live inside rn 1.98. Topaz sits below amethyst for the same reason. What actually predicts brightness is the span of the ratio table, not the symmetry class: log-span against centroid correlates at r = 0.94, while partial count manages only r = 0.60. Symmetry still determines the span — lower symmetry lifts degeneracies and lets modes spread — but it does so loosely enough that chemistry overrides it case by case. Diamond and fluorite are both cubic and sound nothing alike; the entry already said so, and the numbers now agree. The ruby doublet is the clean win: 1.10 and 1.14 over A2 are 121.0 and 125.4 Hz, beating at 4.4 Hz — a slow natural chorus, exactly the shimmer the trigonal hypothesis predicted.

Symmetry Determines Timbre

Diamond (cubic Fd-3m) versus Labradorite (triclinic C-1)

The home entry's core claim is that crystal symmetry shapes timbre. Diamond is the maximum-symmetry pole — four unique partials packed into less than one octave. Labradorite is the minimum-symmetry pole — eight unique partials spread across more than four octaves, with no two intervals symmetry-related. If the claim holds, the same struck additive synth on the same pitch should sound compressed and bright for diamond and scattered and alien for labradorite. It does.

One Crystal, Two Dispersions

Acoustic Birefringence in Emerald

Birefringence in optics — one ray of light enters, two rays leave at different speeds — has a phonon twin. In beryl, sound propagating along the c-axis (down the soft hollow Si₆O₁₈ channels) sees a different dispersion law than sound propagating across the basal plane (through the stiff six-membered silicate rings). Same crystal, same strike, two smears.

Dry clickbroadband strike · undispersed control

What we are teaching A crystal is not isotropic unless its symmetry makes it so. Hexagonal beryl has an optic axis, and that optic axis is also a phonon axis. The dispersion law that determines how a click smears in time is direction-dependent. The two clips are the same physics looked through from two angles — the auditory image of birefringence.

The Crystal as a Prism in Time

Acoustic Dispersion — the Optical-Sonic Bridge

An optical prism separates a white flash in space by frequency. A dispersive crystal filter separates a broadband click in time by frequency. Low frequencies arrive first (zone-centre, fast); high frequencies arrive late (zone-boundary, slow). The undispersed click is one event; the dispersed click is a falling rainbow of arrivals, mass conserved, energy retimed.

Dry clickbroadband strike · before the crystal
Dispersed clickdiamond dispersion law applied · rising arrival
Dispersion sweepdispersion strength swept from 0 → max
What we are teaching Dispersion is the fact that ω(k) is not a straight line. Different frequencies travel at different group velocities — for light through glass, for water on the ocean, for phonons through diamond. The render's filter is an all-pass: magnitudes are untouched, only phase (and so group delay) varies with frequency. The smear you hear is the dispersion curve, played out across the listening window.

Paired Partials Beating Against Themselves

Birefringence as Audible Beating

Calcite splits the light · two indices in one stone — hear it: paired partials.
Calcite's strong birefringence yields two refractive indices for one direction of propagation. In the phonon analogue, that becomes paired partials at slightly different frequencies — and the ear hears the difference as slow beating. Quartz, less birefringent, beats less.

What we are teaching The home entry compresses to twelve words: optical properties of crystals, made audible: birefringence as paired partials beating against themselves. This proof is the haiku's ear-check. The interval between the paired partials is set by the difference of the two sound speeds — for the same crystal, the same direction, two polarisations. Beating is the direct auditory translation of double refraction.
