# The first lesson — sixty seconds

The student has placed two food-flakes on the screen: a kick drum (yellow, top-left) and a saxophone (yellow, lower-right). Beside them, one red sink — the listener.

They press play. The colony starts as a small yellow dot near the kick. They can hear the kick. They cannot hear the sax yet — no tube has reached it.

For about ten seconds the dot extends a single tube outward, hesitating, retracting, extending again. They watch it as if watching a vine grow.

Then the tube finds the sax. The mold thickens it. The sax becomes audible — but late, and softened by the tube's length. The student says *oh*.

For the next thirty seconds, the audio plays unchanged. But the mold is moving. A second tube is exploring outward from the sax-end. The first tube is widening at the kick end and narrowing at the sax end. The student hears the kick get nearer; the sax recedes.

At second fifty-eight the second tube reaches the sax from a different angle — a shortcut. The audio doubles, briefly, then settles as the long path retracts.

The student says: *it's listening.*

That is the lesson. Not "this is how feedback delay networks work." The lesson is: *the system was deciding what to keep, while you were listening to it decide.*

There is no slider for that decision. The colony kept what it used. The student listens differently afterward — not to the sound only, but to the fact that the sound is being routed by something that does not have ears and does not need them.
