# Recipe — Cooking pm into a rhythmic phrase

**The group:** pm. Translation plus reflection. No rotation. The simplest reflection group. Every phrase has a mirror axis — the pattern folds onto itself. What is on one side of the axis is the exact mirror image of the other side.

**The fundamental domain:** one half of the cycle, on one side of the mirror axis. The group reflects it. You write half; symmetry completes the phrase as a mirror.

## Ingredients

- One cycle of 16 sixteenth-note positions
- Divide into two halves: positions 1–8 and 9–16
- One vertical mirror axis at the midpoint (between positions 8 and 9)
- Or: place the mirror at the *start* of the cycle — then positions 1–8 are a mirror of 9–16 counting backward

## Method

1. **Choose which axis.** Most natural for sequencers: mirror at the center. Hit at position n maps to position (17 − n). Hit at 1 → hit at 16. Hit at 3 → hit at 14.
2. **Write 3–4 hits in positions 1–8.**
3. **Mirror: place the exact mirror hits at positions (17 − n) for each n.** The phrase is now bilaterally symmetric.
4. **Loop.** The full cycle is heard: the first half moves forward, the second half moves backward-in-shape.

## What you should hear

pm sounds *still*. The phrase doesn't turn (p3) or balance (p2); it *reflects*. The second half is the first half running backward-in-shape. This creates a sense of arrival: the phrase reaches a point and returns to where it came from. Ceremonial rhythms often live here — the pattern that completes itself, that says *this is a boundary*.

There is no momentum. The mirror cancels it. Whatever drive the first half builds, the second half releases. This is why pm patterns sound heavier, more settled, than p2 or p3. They are self-canceling in the best way.

## Where to break it

Remove one mirror hit — leave the original position but silence its reflection. The bilateral symmetry breaks, and the phrase suddenly lurches: one side is heavier than the other. The imbalance feels dramatic. You have broken the stillness and introduced direction. This is often where drummers land when they want to "open up" a ceremonial pattern — one subtle asymmetry and the whole phrase starts moving.

## What this teaches

pm teaches **what reflection does to time**: it stops momentum. Every bilateral structure — architectural symmetry, a face, a palindrome — shares this physics. The phrase goes somewhere and then un-goes there. The break reveals the stillness was active: the mirror was suppressing direction, not preventing it.

The contrast partner: try p2. Both p2 and pm divide the cycle into two related halves, but p2 pivots (rotation) while pm folds (reflection). Dialogue vs. echo. Hinge vs. mirror.
