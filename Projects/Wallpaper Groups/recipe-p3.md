# Recipe — Cooking p3 into a rhythmic phrase

**The group:** p3. A hexagonal lattice with 3-fold rotation centers only. No reflection axes. No glide reflection. Three symmetry operations. The most *angular* of the pure-rotation groups.

**The fundamental domain:** one third of a cycle. Whatever length the cycle is — one bar, 12/8, a 12-step Euclidean grid — you control one-third. The group spins it twice more.

## Ingredients

- One cycle of 12 sixteenth-note positions (or 12 steps in a sequencer)
- Divide into three sectors of 4 steps each: positions 1–4, 5–8, 9–12
- Three rotation axes at positions 1, 5, and 9

## Method

1. **Write 2–3 hits in positions 1–4 only.** Don't worry about the rest. Don't loop it yet.
2. **Copy to positions 5–8, shifting each hit by +4 steps** (mod 12). The rotation.
3. **Copy again to positions 9–12, shifting by +8 steps.** Now the cycle is full and rotationally symmetric.
4. Loop for 8 or 16 bars.

## What you should hear

Unlike p4mm's snowflake stiffness, p3 *grooves*. The three-fold rotation creates a kind of asymmetric rolling pressure — each third arrives at the same accent-shape but from a slightly different position in the listener's expectation. It doesn't feel frozen; it feels like it's turning. West African polyrhythm uses exactly this structure (three against twelve), as do many Carnatic tāla subdivisions.

The absence of reflection is key. Because p3 has rotation but no mirror, the phrase doesn't have a *mirror partner* canceling its thrust. It simply turns.

## Where to break it

Break the symmetry at one rotation. Replace one hit in the second sector with a rest, or add an accent outside the pattern in the third sector. Because p3 has no reflection to cancel the break, the breach bleeds further than in p4mm — it changes the feel of the whole cycle, not just a local moment.

## What this teaches

p3 teaches the difference between **rotation symmetry** and **reflection symmetry** as groove physics. Reflection flattens; rotation propels. The Alhambra's artisans knew this in their hands. A p3 tiling has momentum; a p4mm tiling sits still.

Try p6 next (6-fold rotation, no reflection): the momentum doubles, the cycle compresses, and you approach the territory of fast West African bell patterns. Each rotation group has a different groove character. *That character is the break-vocabulary.*
