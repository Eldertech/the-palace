# Meadows and an Artist's Career

A pivot of the original [[Meadows and Music — Leverage Points]] project. The original applied Donella Meadows' twelve leverage points to *music production* — sidechain compression as negative feedback, transient design as buffer-size manipulation. That work is real and lives on in its own entry.

This entry is the *second pass* — the same hierarchy applied to the system that produces music: **the artist's career as a system**. Where do you put your energy? What kind of intervention is "buy a new compressor" versus "rewrite the goals of my practice"? Meadows' answer: it depends on where in the leverage hierarchy you intervene, and most artists waste their lives at the bottom of it.

## The Twelve Leverage Points — Career Edition

Working from Meadows' 1999 paper, lowest-leverage to highest:

### 12 — Constants and parameters (lowest leverage)
*"How much do I charge per show? Should I switch to a different DAW?"*
This is where most artists spend most of their energy. Fiddling with parameters of an existing system. Changing the gear, the pricing, the calendar. These changes feel productive because they're tangible. They produce small improvements. They almost never change the trajectory of a career.

### 11 — Buffer sizes
*"How much money should I keep saved? How many gigs ahead should I be booked?"*
The size of your slack. Three months of savings vs three weeks. Two albums in the can vs none. Buffers absorb shocks; without them, every shock becomes a crisis. With them, you can survive losing a regular gig long enough to find a better one.

### 10 — Structure of stocks and flows
*"What is the ratio of paid work to creative work? What is the ratio of teaching to making?"*
The plumbing of your career. How money comes in and what it funds. How attention comes in and what it produces. Restructuring this — for example, shifting from "I teach to fund my recording" to "I record to fund my teaching" — is a much bigger move than tweaking either flow's parameters.

### 9 — Delays in the system
*"How long does it take me to ship something? How long is the gap between deciding and starting?"*
The career's response time. Some artists ship monthly; some ship every five years. The longer the delay, the harder it is to course-correct. Reducing delays — by shortening the cycle from idea to release — fundamentally changes how much learning the system can do per year.

### 8 — Balancing feedback loops
*"What stops me when I'm going too far? What protects me from burnout?"*
Negative feedback in your career — the mechanisms that bring you back from extremes. A partner who notices you're not sleeping. A practice of weekly reflection. A daily walk. Without balancing loops, success eats itself.

### 7 — Reinforcing feedback loops
*"What pulls me forward when I'm gaining momentum? What makes the next step easier than the last?"*
Positive feedback. An audience that builds. A skill that compounds. A reputation that opens doors. Identifying where you have reinforcing loops in your career — and protecting them — is one of the highest-leverage things an artist can do, because reinforcing loops are how careers actually grow non-linearly.

### 6 — Information flows
*"Who tells me what's actually happening? What metric do I trust?"*
A career runs on the information available to it. If you only see Spotify streams, you're optimizing for streams. If you only hear from fans, you're optimizing for fans. Adding a missing information flow — a friend who tells you the truth, a metric that tracks something deeper than streams — can change where the whole system points.

### 5 — Rules of the system
*"What can I say no to? What kinds of work won't I take?"*
The constraints you place on yourself. *I do not take work that requires me to lie about what I make. I do not collaborate with people whose values I cannot defend.* Rules are how a system protects what matters. The right rule clarifies; the wrong rule strangles.

### 4 — Self-organization
*"How do I keep changing my own structure?"*
The capacity to redesign yourself. Adding a new skill is parameter change (level 12). Letting that skill change the kinds of work you take is structural change. Letting the structure of work-you-take change your identity — *I used to be a producer; I've become an instrument designer* — is self-organization. Most artists' careers can self-organize at most twice in a lifetime; some never do.

### 3 — Goals of the system
*"What is this career FOR?"*
The hardest one to answer honestly. Most career advice optimizes a goal you didn't choose. *Make a living from music* and *make music that matters* and *be famous* are different goals; they produce different careers. The goal determines everything downstream. Most stagnation in artists' careers is stagnation at the goal level — they're optimizing brilliantly for a goal that is no longer theirs.

### 2 — Paradigms — the mindset out of which the system arises
*"What does it mean to be an artist?"*
The deeper-than-goal: *the kind of human a person who does this work is becoming*. A musician trained inside the paradigm "music is content" produces different music than a musician trained inside "music is a way of paying attention." The paradigm precedes the goals. Changing it is rare and disorienting and almost always involuntary — usually triggered by crisis, illness, loss, or radical new exposure.

### 1 — The power to transcend paradigms (highest leverage)
*"Letting go of any single answer to 'what is this career FOR'."*
The artist who has done this multiple times — who has entered, lived, and exited several paradigms about what their work is — develops a kind of mobility that lower-leverage operators can't access. They don't just have a career; they can have several, sequentially or simultaneously. This is the leverage point Meadows describes as "transcendence" — not enlightenment, just the capacity to hold any answer to "what's it all for" with both hands and put it down when it stops being true.

## The Diagnostic

Most artists I know spend roughly:

- **80%** of their attention at level 12 (parameter tweaking — gear, pricing, scheduling)
- **15%** at levels 8–11 (managing buffers, ratios, delays — the operational layer)
- **4%** at levels 5–7 (rules, feedback loops, information flows)
- **1%** at levels 1–4 (self-organization, goals, paradigms)

The leverage is inverted. The ratio of attention to leverage is upside down. Most career advice keeps you at the parameter level because parameter-level moves are legible and shareable; paradigm-level moves are private, slow, and hard to teach.

This project's job is to make the higher-leverage moves *legible enough to attempt*.
