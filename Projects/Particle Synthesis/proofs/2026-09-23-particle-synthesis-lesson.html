Particle Synthesis · sketch · 2026-09-23

A fountain on ice

What if every grain in a granular synth were a real particle — thrown, falling, bouncing — and the sound came from the physics of where it landed rather than from knobs? Let's walk the arc of this project in seven moves, then play with the thing it has become.

01The idea

A granular synth chops sound into tiny grains and scatters them. Usually each grain is a scheduled event with a pitch, a length and an envelope, all set by parameters. Particle Synthesis asks the grain to be a thing instead: a particle with position, velocity and mass, moved by the same kind of physics a game engine uses for sparks and rain.

The test the project set itself is strict. It should sound like physics, not parameter modulation — the kind of sound your ear files under "something happened in a real place," not "an LFO is sweeping a filter."

03The law under the ice

Strike a frozen lake with a stone and you hear a descending pew. Bending waves in a stiff plate travel at a speed that depends on their frequency: high frequencies outrun low ones. A strike contains every frequency at once, so by the time it reaches you it has been sorted into a sweep, highs first. That sorting is dispersion, and it is the whole sound.

the plate's dispersion law
ω = C · k²
(angular frequency) = (plate stiffness) × (wavenumber)²
when each frequency arrives
τ(ω) = r ⁄ (2 · √(C · ω))
(arrival time of a frequency) = (distance to the pickup) ⁄ (2 × √((plate stiffness) × (angular frequency)))
turned around — the pitch you hear at each instant
ω(t) = r² ⁄ (4 · C · t²)
(pitch heard at time t) = (distance)² ⁄ (4 × (plate stiffness) × (time since the strike)²)

That last line is the one to keep. It says a strike heard from farther away sweeps more slowly: double the distance and the chirp takes twice as long. Try it.

top: when each frequency arrives. bottom: the sound itself. heavier = longer contact = darker strike.

04The fountain — play it

Now the particles. An emitter throws them upward; gravity and a little air drag bring them down; they skip across the plate, losing a bit more than half their bounce each time. Every strike is heard through the law above, at both pickups. Walk the fountain away from the pickups and listen to the chirps stretch. Switch the ice off and the same strikes become plain clicks — that difference is what dispersion adds.

05The offline renders

The same model rendered exactly in Python, frequency by frequency, with the fountain walking from 3 m to 18 m away over ten seconds. The browser above uses a closed-form shortcut for the chirp; checked against the exact render on twenty strikes it matches with a correlation of 0.997 or better, so what you play here is the same sound, just computed differently.

The page's own number. 5,005 strikes. Density turns the chirps into weather.

Each file is peak-normalised on its own. Dispersion only moves energy in time, never adds or removes it, so the dry and iced clouds carry the same energy — the clicks just spend it all in an instant, which is why 01 sounds thinner at the same peak.

06Grains that meet

Until now every grain has fallen alone. A thousand of them only add up, like rain, and none of them knows the others are there. The page always asked the harder question: what happens when grains interact rather than superpose? So let's aim two fountains at each other. They stand 3 m apart and fire together, and the grains are small balls of ice, a centimetre or two across. When two meet in the air they bounce apart, and you hear that meeting through the air. When a grain lands, you hear it through the ice, as before.

The clack is not a sample. Two ice balls touch for about a tenth of a millisecond (Hertz's law for two spheres, with ice's real stiffness), so the clack is bright and dry. Each ball gets shoved and pushes on the air like a tiny loudspeaker. The two are shoved in opposite directions, so their sounds cancel off to the side and add up along the line of impact. Nobody coded that pattern. It comes out of adding the two balls together.

how long two ice balls touch · Hertz
T꜀ = 2.87 · ( m² ⁄ (R · E*² · v) )^(1/5)
(contact time) = 2.87 × ( (paired mass)² ⁄ ((paired radius) × (ice stiffness)² × (closing speed)) )^(1/5)
the clack at a microphone · two balls, pushed apart
p(t) = Σ ± (3ρₐ ⁄ (8π ρᵢ c)) · (cos θ ⁄ r) · dF⁄dt (t − r⁄c)
(pressure) = sum over both balls of ± (3 × air density ⁄ (8π × ice density × speed of sound)) × (cos(angle off the impact line) ⁄ distance) × (rate of change of the contact force, delayed by distance ⁄ speed of sound)

Tighten the aim and listen. With loose aim the streams mostly pass through each other and only land. As the aim closes, meetings thicken, deflected grains land in new places, and now and then a grain bouncing back knocks the next one straight up. Switch to pass through and the same fountains go back to rain.

Two honest readings of that table. First, the meetings did not change the average chirp length. The fountains are mirror images, so a head-on meeting just swaps which side each grain lands on. What they did change is the spread: landings drift sideways four times as far, which widens the stereo picture across the two pickups. Second, aim is the knob that sets how much the grains interact. Centimetre grains thrown 1.7 m need aim inside about half a degree to meet reliably. That is a real finding about the instrument, not a setting I chose.

Grains that ring

Solid ice balls ring far above hearing, so a meeting is only a tick. Hollow shells ring low enough to hear. Give each grain its own small resonator and a meeting gets a pitch set by the grain's size. That is the page's first forward direction: a particle carrying its own differential equation.

needs the Mac

A chirp oscillator in Gen~

The closed form means no FFT per grain: each strike is one oscillator whose pitch follows r² ⁄ (4Ct²). That is a per-sample voice Gen~ can run, with the particle carrying its own state — the page's first forward direction.

needs a GPU

The compute shader

A thousand particles stepping physics, each strike a voice summed into one signal — the parallel shape the page has pointed at from the start. The Python render took about 34 seconds of CPU for 12 seconds of the 1000-particle cloud; that is the number to beat.
