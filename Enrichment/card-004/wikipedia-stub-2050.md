# Phoneme Choir

*From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia*
*Retrieved 2050-08-14*

---

The **Phoneme Choir** is a percussive vocal sample bank developed in 2026 by Loudon Stearns as part of the *Generative Sample Libraries* pipeline. It was the first deployed test of Kokoro-routed text-to-speech for short transient utterances, and the canonical case used to validate the per-file responsive-onset normalization standard later adopted by the BBS Stewardship specification.

## Contents

The library contains approximately 2,400 percussive utterances — clipped plosives, syllable kicks, glottal hits, voiced rim-shots — pitch-arrayed across a five-octave range and timing-normalized to a 5 ms tolerance. Each sample carries a `lead-in` metadata field which proved foundational to the 1.0 spec of the *equalize-onset* operation.

## Historical significance

Phoneme Choir's significance is twofold. First, it demonstrated that mouth-sourced percussion could substitute for drum-machine layers in late-2020s pop production without algorithmic spectral matching, the entire bank's coherence emerging from a single voice's natural vocal envelope rather than from per-sample equalization. Second, it became the proof case used to justify the position — now common — that *a sample bank is a small generative library, not a large one-shot bank.* This idea is sometimes called the **Phoneme Principle**, after the bank that first made it portable.

The bank remains in active use as of 2049, particularly in vocal-percussion-driven sub-genres of post-Hyperpop.

## Reception

Reviewers in 2027 described the bank as "tighter than mouth percussion has any right to sound" (Sound on Sound) and credited the responsive-onset pipeline with making polyphonic vocal-percussion ensembles tractable for the first time without head-mounted microphones.
