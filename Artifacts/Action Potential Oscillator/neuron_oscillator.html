The Action Potential Oscillator

Designing an audio oscillator from neuronal first principles — biology, mathematics, and synthesis in four progressive stages

Music Technology Research Document · 2026

Introduction: Architecture of a Neuron-Oscillator

A single action potential cycle has distinct phases, and a neuron is always in exactly one of them. This maps naturally to a state machine — clean to implement in Gen~ and compilable through RNBO because it uses only primitive operations: comparison, arithmetic, and conditional assignment.

The four phases of our oscillator's cycle, mapped directly to their biological origins:

PhaseBiologyWaveform Region
1 — IntegrationSynaptic input accumulates on membrane capacitance; leak conductance drains chargeRising ramp
2 — DepolarizationNa⁺ channels explode open; membrane rockets toward +40 mVSharp spike peak
3 — RepolarizationNa⁺ inactivation + K⁺ activation; voltage crashes through restDownstroke & undershoot
4 — RefractoryNa⁺ inactivation gates reset; K⁺ conductance decays; return to restDead zone → recovery

The total time through all four phases determines the oscillator's period, and therefore its pitch. We will build each stage progressively, beginning with the simplest possible model and adding biological realism layer by layer.

Stage 1Linear Integrate-and-Fire: The Capacitor

The Biology

Strip away everything about a neuron except the most basic fact: the cell membrane is a capacitor. It separates charge. The lipid bilayer, roughly 7–8 nm thick, acts as a dielectric between the conducting intracellular and extracellular fluids. Its specific capacitance is approximately 1 µF/cm², remarkably consistent across all cell types — a value that arises directly from the dielectric constant of the lipid membrane and its thickness [1].

When current flows into the cell (through synaptic input or, in our oscillator, through a "drive current" that controls pitch), charge accumulates on the membrane, and voltage rises. When voltage reaches a threshold (~−55 mV in a typical cortical neuron, approximately 15 mV above rest), something dramatic happens. Then the system resets.

Synthesis Connection
This is the same principle as charging a capacitor through a resistor in an analog synth's ramp oscillator. Analog oscillator cores based on capacitor charging and comparator-triggered reset are performing a simplified version of what a neuron does.

The Math

For a pure capacitor with no leak:

Cm × dV/dt = Iinput
Cm
Membrane capacitance (~1 µF/cm² — the lipid bilayer's ability to store charge)
dV/dt
Rate of change of membrane voltage over time (what we hear as the waveform)
Iinput
Injected current — in our oscillator, the "drive" that controls pitch

This gives a linear charging rate: dV/dt = Iinput / Cm. Voltage increases at a constant rate, and the time to reach threshold Vth from a reset value Vreset is:

f = Iinput / (Cm × (Vth − Vreset))
f
Oscillation frequency — directly proportional to input current
Vth
Threshold voltage (normalized to 1.0 in our system)
Vreset
Reset voltage after spike (normalized to 0.0)

Frequency is directly proportional to input current. This is the simplest possible relationship, and it's actually a reasonable first approximation of the real neuronal f–I (frequency-current) curve in the suprathreshold regime.

This is literally a sawtooth wave built from the neuron's perspective. Linear charge-to-threshold-and-reset produces an ascending ramp that snaps back to zero, containing all harmonics with amplitudes falling as 1/n. Bright, buzzy, familiar.

Spatial variation — we treat the neuron as a single point (the standard "point neuron" assumption).

// STAGE 1: Linear Integrate-and-Fire // Output: sawtooth-like ramp from 0 to 1, then reset param frequency = 440; History membrane_v = 0; charge_increment = frequency / samplerate; membrane_v = membrane_v + charge_increment; if (membrane_v >= 1.0) { membrane_v = membrane_v - 1.0; // wrap preserves phase accuracy } out1 = membrane_v * 2.0 - 1.0; // scale to ±1 audio range

Stage 2Leaky Integrate-and-Fire: The RC Circuit

The Biology

Now we add the most fundamental electrical property of the neuronal membrane: it leaks. The lipid bilayer is not a perfect insulator. Even at rest, potassium ions slowly diffuse outward through leak channels (sometimes called "background" or "resting" potassium channels). The resting membrane potential of approximately −70 mV exists because K⁺ permeability dominates — about 20 times greater than Na⁺ permeability at rest [2, 3]. This passive leak constantly pulls the membrane potential back toward the potassium equilibrium potential (EK ≈ −90 mV).

The following ion concentrations establish the electrochemical gradients that drive all neural signaling:

IonIntracellular (mM)Extracellular (mM)EequilRole
K⁺~140~5−90 mVSets resting potential; repolarizes spike
Na⁺~12~145+60 mVDrives depolarization spike
Cl⁻~10~110−80 mVInhibitory; stabilizes resting potential
Ca²⁺~0.0001~2+125 mVSignaling; synaptic release trigger
Ion concentration values: Purves et al., Neuroscience, 2nd ed. (Sinauer, 2001) [4]; equilibrium potentials: StatPearls, "Physiology, Action Potential" (NCBI, 2023) [5]; ScienceDirect, "Nerve Cell Membrane Potential" [6].

These gradients are maintained by the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase pump, which actively transports 3 Na⁺ out and 2 K⁺ in per cycle, consuming one ATP molecule per cycle. This pump's activity accounts for an estimated 20–40% of the brain's total energy budget [4], and sodium pumping overall is responsible for the majority of the brain's ATP consumption [7].

The Nernst Equation

The equilibrium potential for any single ion — the voltage at which the electrical force exactly balances the concentration gradient — is given by:

Eion = (RT / zF) × ln([ion]out / [ion]in)
Eion
Equilibrium potential for a given ion species (mV)
R
Universal gas constant — 8.314 J·K⁻¹·mol⁻¹ (relates thermal energy to molecular quantity)
T
Absolute temperature in Kelvin (310 K for mammalian body temp = 37°C)
z
Ion valence (+1 for K⁺/Na⁺, +2 for Ca²⁺, −1 for Cl⁻)
F
Faraday's constant — 96,485 C·mol⁻¹ (charge carried by one mole of electrons)
ln
Natural logarithm of the outside-to-inside concentration ratio

At mammalian body temperature (37°C), the factor RT/zF ≈ 26.7 mV for monovalent ions, giving approximately 61 mV per tenfold concentration change when converted to log base 10 [8]. For K⁺ with a roughly 28:1 inside-to-outside ratio, EK ≈ −90 mV. For Na⁺ with roughly 12:1 outside-to-inside ratio, ENa ≈ +60 mV [5].

The Leak Equation

Electrically, adding leak gives us an RC circuit. The membrane capacitance Cm in parallel with the leak resistance Rm gives a time constant τ = Rm × Cm, typically 10–30 ms in cortical neurons [9]. Charge placed on the capacitor exponentially decays back to the resting potential with this time constant:

dV/dt = −V/τ + Idrive
V/τ
Leak term — voltage (relative to rest) divided by membrane time constant. This is the passive K⁺ leak constantly pulling voltage back toward rest.
Idrive
Input drive current (controls pitch). Must overcome the leak to reach threshold.
τ
Membrane time constant = Rm × Cm (typically 10–30 ms in cortex). Determines how quickly charge dissipates.

The steady-state voltage (where dV/dt = 0) is V∞ = Idrive × τ. The neuron can only fire if V∞ > Vthreshold, meaning the drive current must be strong enough to overcome the leak. This creates a minimum current for spiking (the rheobase), which is biologically real and creates a natural low-frequency limit for our oscillator.

The exact inter-spike interval with exponential charging:

Tcharge = −τ × ln(1 − Vth / V∞)
Tcharge
Time from reset to threshold crossing — determines oscillator period
τ
Membrane time constant
Vth
Threshold voltage (normalized to 1.0)
V∞
Steady-state voltage = Idrive × τ (must exceed Vth for spiking to occur)
ln
Natural logarithm — this makes the f–I curve nonlinear
Synthesis Connection
The discrete-time leak is a one-pole lowpass filter applied to membrane voltage: V[n+1] = V[n] × (1 − dt/τ) + Idrive × dt. The term (1 − dt/τ) is the leak coefficient — exactly the same exponential smoothing coefficient you use in attack/release smoothing in compressors and envelope followers. The membrane of a neuron IS doing the same math as a one-pole filter.

Voltage-dependent leak — even "leak" channels (KCNK two-pore K⁺ channels) have mild outward rectification, leaking more at depolarized potentials. Pseudocode — Stage 2

// STAGE 2: Leaky Integrate-and-Fire // Output: exponential ramp (concave curve) from 0 to threshold param frequency = 440; param leak_tau_ms = 5; leak_tau = leak_tau_ms * 0.001 * samplerate; leak_coeff = 1.0 - (1.0 / leak_tau); period_samples = samplerate / frequency; drive = 1.0 / (leak_tau * (1.0 - exp(-period_samples / leak_tau))); History membrane_v = 0; membrane_v = membrane_v * leak_coeff + drive; if (membrane_v >= 1.0) { membrane_v = 0; } out1 = membrane_v * 2.0 - 1.0;

Stage 3Shaped Spike: The Action Potential Waveform

The Biology

Until now, our "spike" has been an instantaneous reset. But the real action potential is a shaped event with its own internal dynamics lasting roughly 1–2 milliseconds. During the spike, two populations of channels are racing against each other: Na⁺ activation gates (m) snap open with a time constant of roughly 0.1–0.3 ms, while Na⁺ inactivation gates (h) close with a time constant of roughly 0.5–1 ms, and K⁺ activation gates (n) open with a time constant of roughly 1–3 ms [1, 10].

The spike's shape is determined by the relative speeds of these three gating processes, described by the Hodgkin-Huxley equations:

Cm × dV/dt = −g̅Na m³h(V − ENa) − g̅K n⁴(V − EK) − gL(V − EL) + Iext
Cm
Membrane capacitance (~1 µF/cm²)
g̅Na m³h
Sodium conductance: maximum conductance × 3 activation gates × 1 inactivation gate (all must be open simultaneously)
g̅K n⁴
Potassium conductance: maximum conductance × 4 activation gates (all must be open)
gL
Leak conductance (constant — chloride and other ions)
ENa, EK, EL
Reversal (Nernst) potentials for each ion: +60 mV, −90 mV, ~−65 mV
m, h, n
Gating variables (0–1 probability that each molecular gate is open), each following first-order voltage-dependent kinetics
Hodgkin, A.L. & Huxley, A.F. "A quantitative description of membrane current and its application to conduction and excitation in nerve." J. Physiol. 117: 500–544 (1952). Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine, 1963 [1].

Each gating variable follows first-order kinetics where it relaxes toward a voltage-dependent steady state:

dm/dt = αm(V) × (1 − m) − βm(V) × m
dm/dt
Rate of change of sodium activation gate probability
αm(V)
Voltage-dependent opening rate — increases sharply with depolarization
βm(V)
Voltage-dependent closing rate
m
Current probability that an activation gate is open (0 to 1)

For our MVP oscillator, we don't simulate the full Hodgkin-Huxley system. Instead, we prescribe a spike waveform using paired exponentials that capture the essential asymmetry: fast rise (Na⁺ activation, ~0.1 ms) and slower fall (Na⁺ inactivation + K⁺ activation, ~0.5–1 ms). This is legitimate computational neuroscience — the "fire-and-reset with spike insertion" method — because the spike shape is highly stereotyped (nearly identical every time, hence the "all-or-nothing" law).

Spike Duration and Pitch

The spike has a fixed biological duration (~1–2 ms regardless of firing rate). As oscillator frequency rises, the spike occupies an increasing fraction of each cycle. At 100 Hz, a 1 ms spike is 10% of the period. At 1000 Hz, it's 100%. This creates a natural maximum frequency — biologically accurate (real neurons max out at ~500–1000 Hz for fast-spiking interneurons) [11] and sonically interesting as the character changes with pitch.

Stage 4Hyperpolarization & Refractory Period: The Full MVP

The Biology

After the spike's repolarization, the membrane potential doesn't just return neatly to rest. It overshoots to the negative side, typically reaching −80 to −85 mV versus a resting potential of −70 mV. This afterhyperpolarization (AHP) occurs because K⁺ channels opened during the spike are still conducting outward current. Since the potassium Nernst potential is approximately −90 mV, the elevated K⁺ conductance pulls the membrane below rest, toward EK [5, 10].

During the AHP, the neuron is in its refractory period. In the absolute refractory period (~1–2 ms after the spike), Na⁺ inactivation gates have not yet reset — no stimulus of any strength can trigger another spike. In the relative refractory period (next ~2–5 ms), Na⁺ gates are partially recovered but the hyperpolarized membrane and residual K⁺ conductance demand a stronger-than-normal stimulus [10, 11].

Synthesis Connection — Damped Oscillator
The recovery from hyperpolarization in many neurons exhibits damped ringing caused by the interplay between recovering Na⁺ current and decaying K⁺ current. This is the mass-spring-damper system: the "spring" is the combined ion channel forces pulling toward equilibrium, the "mass" is membrane capacitance, and the "damping" is leak conductance. Depending on parameters: overdamped (smooth exponential return), critically damped (fastest non-oscillatory return), or underdamped (decaying oscillation with gamma-band ringing).

What This Sounds Like
This is no longer any standard waveform. Each cycle has four distinct timbral regions: a warm exponential ramp (low-to-mid harmonics), a sharp transient spike (broad high-frequency energy), a rapid descent into negative territory (harmonic cancellation at specific frequencies), and a damped recovery that may ring (sub-harmonic content). The character sits between a sawtooth, a pulse wave, and something entirely its own — a bright, organic buzz with an embedded transient click and a subtle negative "breath" after each spike.

Complete Inventory: Future Nonlinearities

Affects Spike Shape (spectral content per cycle)

1. Sodium activation cubic nonlinearity (m³) — the autocatalytic positive feedback explosion that makes the rising edge near-vertical. 2. Sodium inactivation dynamics (h gate) — shapes the peak's curvature and rise-to-fall transition. 3. Potassium delayed rectifier (n⁴) — sigmoid K⁺ onset shapes the falling edge. 4. Temperature dependence — all rate constants have Q₁₀ factors; "temperature" as a global dynamics macro.

Affects Cycle-to-Cycle Variation (timbral evolution)

5. Spike amplitude adaptation — incomplete Na⁺ inactivation recovery at fast rates reduces spike amplitude, naturally darkening timbre at high frequencies. 6. Calcium-dependent K⁺ currents — slow AHP (100–1000 ms) causing spike-frequency adaptation: starts fast, gradually slows. 7. Stochastic channel gating — probabilistic opening/closing adds pitch jitter and noise whose character depends on simulated channel count.

Affects Modular Interaction (ecosystem connections)

8. Synaptic input modeling — multiple "synapse" inputs with distinct dynamics (fast AMPA, slow NMDA, inhibitory GABA). 9. Short-term synaptic plasticity — input-dependent timbral variation through facilitation and depression. 10. Gap junction coupling — direct electrical coupling between oscillator instances for emergent synchronization behavior.
