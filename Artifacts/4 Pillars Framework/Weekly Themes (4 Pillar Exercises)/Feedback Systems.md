# Feedback Systems
## When the Output Becomes the Input

**Difficulty:** Deep | **Philosopher:** Norbert Wiener

In 1943, mathematician Norbert Wiener was working on anti-aircraft gun predictors for the Allied forces — systems that needed to anticipate the future position of a moving target rather than aim at its present position. The problem required modeling the gun, the aircraft, and the human operator together as a single dynamic system, each component receiving information from the others and adjusting in response. Out of this wartime engineering problem, Wiener extracted something far more general: the concept of cybernetics — the study of communication and control in systems that use feedback to regulate themselves. A thermostat regulates temperature by measuring the difference between the actual temperature and the desired temperature, using that difference as the input to a corrective action, measuring again, adjusting again. This loop — compare actual to desired, use the error as control signal, repeat — is feedback. Wiener's insight was that the same logic governs thermostats, neurons, ecosystems, economies, and the human arm reaching for a glass of water. Every goal-seeking system, biological or mechanical, is a feedback system. In music, feedback is both a practical reality and a compositional principle. A guitar placed too close to its amplifier begins to sustain indefinitely, then to howl, then to produce complex overtone clouds as the loop system enters nonlinear territory. Pete Townshend and Jimi Hendrix didn't fight feedback — they understood it as a second instrument, one that responded to position, gain, and the acoustic properties of the room. In synthesis, feedback loops are designed deliberately: in FM synthesis with feedback operators, in voltage-controlled oscillators patched into their own frequency inputs, in resonant filters approaching self-oscillation. This week you will build three tracks in which feedback is the compositional engine — not an effect but a structural principle — build a feedback delay system with a visual state display, and read Wiener's foundational text on what feedback systems actually are and why they matter far beyond music.

## The Four Pillars Integration

### Creation (Music Production)
**Assignment:** Build three tracks in which feedback is the primary compositional mechanism — not an effect applied to finished material but the generator of musical structure.

**Track 1: Controlled Feedback Decay**
- Start with a single drum loop or melodic phrase. Route it through a delay unit with 100% feedback (infinite loop) and a high-pass filter in the feedback path.
- Each repetition passes through the filter, progressively losing low frequencies. The loop decays spectrally even as it repeats rhythmically — the system is self-consuming.
- At a chosen moment, reduce the feedback below 100%: the loop begins to decay. Manage the exit as carefully as you manage the entry.
- Notice: the high-pass filter in the feedback path is a law governing evolution. Change the filter and you change what the system becomes.

**Track 2: Feedback as Structure**
- Use a resonant filter (resonance near self-oscillation) as the primary melodic voice. Set the filter frequency by playing notes through it — the resonance sustains the pitch after the transient has passed.
- Route MIDI pitch data to the filter cutoff frequency so you can "play" the resonance as an instrument.
- Build a track where the melodic content is entirely generated by filter self-oscillation: the frequencies are real, the pitches are precise, but the source is a loop, not an oscillator.
- Notice: the instrument does not generate sound — it amplifies the frequency at which it resonates. The music comes from the system's preference.

**Track 3: Uncontrolled Feedback as Texture**
- Set up genuine, destabilized feedback: a microphone input feeding into a speaker at a gain just above unity, positioned in a room where the acoustic feedback tone can be shaped by microphone position.
- Or: in a modular system, patch an oscillator output into its own frequency input through an attenuator. Modulate the attenuator.
- Record the results. Edit and structure them.
- Notice: at what gain does the system become uncontrollable? Is the transition abrupt or gradual? What does the edge of control feel like as a compositional space?

**Technical Focus:**
- Feedback gain: the single parameter that determines whether a feedback system converges (gain < 1), sustains (gain = 1), or diverges (gain > 1). Most musical feedback lives at the edge between 1 and just-above-1.
- Feedback path processing: every element in the feedback loop (filter, delay time, pitch shift, reverb) shapes the evolution of the system. The loop's processing is its constitution.
- Phase and delay: the delay time in a feedback loop determines the frequency at which the loop constructively reinforces itself (the fundamental) and its harmonics. This is why feedback on a guitar produces a specific pitch determined by the distance between guitar and amplifier.
- Nonlinear feedback: when gain is significantly above unity, feedback systems enter nonlinear regimes — saturation, distortion, chaos. The DX7's feedback operator and the screaming guitar both live here.

### Tools (Build With AI)

**Concept:** A feedback delay visualizer — a browser-based delay system with adjustable feedback and filter, showing the system's state (the current contents of the delay buffer) as a visual waveform that evolves in real time. The goal is to see the feedback loop as a living object.

**Start here:** Ask an AI: *"Help me build a browser-based audio feedback delay visualizer. I want: an audio input (or a looping sample player), a delay unit with three controls: delay time (10–1000ms), feedback (0–110%), and a high-pass filter cutoff in the feedback path (20Hz–2000Hz). Display the delay buffer contents as a real-time waveform. Include a level meter showing gain in the feedback loop. When feedback exceeds 100%, the system should diverge — show this visually: the waveform amplitude grows until it clips. Add a 'reset' button that clears the buffer."*

Run your Track 1 or Track 2 patch through this system while watching the buffer visualization. See the feedback loop as a living waveform rather than a knob setting. This is Wiener's thermostat made visible: the output becoming the input, the state of the system at each moment determining the state at the next.

### Philosophy (Cybernetics and Self-Regulation)
**Reading:** Norbert Wiener — *The Human Use of Human Beings* (1950) — the popular account of cybernetics, written for a general audience, covering feedback, communication, information, and the ethics of self-regulating systems

**Key Context:**
Wiener is addressing the deepest question about systems that can regulate themselves: what is the relationship between information, purpose, and control? The thermostat "wants" to maintain a target temperature — but that word, *wants*, is a metaphor that Wiener takes seriously. A goal-seeking system behaves *as if* it has a goal: it receives information about the difference between its current state and its target state, and it acts to reduce that difference. Whether the system is a thermostat, a neuron, an immune response, or a government — the structure is the same.

**The Feedback Equation:**
Every feedback system has: a *setpoint* (the goal), a *sensor* (which measures the actual state), a *comparator* (which computes the error: actual minus desired), and an *actuator* (which acts to reduce the error). Music's feedback systems often lack an explicit setpoint — the feedback loop evolves without a target, producing behavior that is neither random nor controlled. This is the edge between cybernetics and chaos theory.

**Negative vs. Positive Feedback:**
*Negative feedback* acts to reduce error — the thermostat turns off the heat when the room is warm enough. It stabilizes. *Positive feedback* amplifies deviation — the guitar amp howling louder as its output drives higher gain. It destabilizes. Most complex systems use both: positive feedback to grow or change direction, negative feedback to regulate and stabilize. A fern growing toward light is using positive feedback (more light → more growth toward light) regulated by negative feedback (structural limits on stem length, resource constraints). The musical question is: which kind of feedback are you composing with?

**Discussion Questions:**
- Wiener writes that the distinguishing feature of living things is not their material composition but their *organization* — specifically, their capacity to maintain themselves against entropy through feedback. Does this claim hold up? What does it mean for a musical system?
- A resonant filter approaching self-oscillation "wants" to sustain a particular frequency. In what sense is that a goal? Is the goal in the filter or in our description of it?
- Wiener was deeply concerned about the social implications of cybernetics — specifically, that feedback-controlled automation would displace human labor without human institutions being prepared to manage the consequences. This concern is now 75 years old. How accurate was his prediction?

### Practice (Creative Wellbeing)
**Daily Practice:** Map your feedback loops

For one week, notice the feedback loops operating in your own life — not as systems to optimize, but as structures to observe.

Some examples: a creative practice that generates energy the more you do it (positive feedback). A habit that generates guilt, which leads to more of the habit (positive feedback in the wrong direction). A relationship where honest communication reduces tension, which makes honest communication easier (negative feedback, stabilizing). A work environment where overwork produces errors that require more work to fix (positive feedback, destabilizing).

**The Feedback Question:**
For each loop you identify: What is the setpoint (if there is one)? What is the sensor (how does the system measure its state)? What is the error signal (what gets fed back)? Is the feedback negative (stabilizing) or positive (amplifying)?

**Weekly Reflection:**
"Which feedback loop in my creative life is currently near unity gain — at the edge between sustaining and diverging? What would it sound like if that loop were a guitar approaching an amplifier? What would the feedback tone be?"

## Cross-Domain Resonance

### Feedback Systems ↔ Learning
Every time you practice a skill, receive information about your performance (feedback), and adjust your approach, you are running a cybernetic loop. The faster and more accurate the feedback, the faster the learning. This is why deliberate practice (specific, immediate, corrective feedback) is more effective than simple repetition (practice without feedback). Wiener's thermostat and the musician practicing scales are running the same algorithm.

### Feedback Systems ↔ Markets
A market price is a feedback signal: when the price of a commodity rises, production increases and consumption decreases (negative feedback, stabilizing toward equilibrium). When a stock rises and draws more buyers (positive feedback), the price rises further until the loop breaks. Economic booms and crashes are positive feedback loops entering and exiting nonlinear regimes. The resonant filter approaching self-oscillation and the 2008 financial crisis are structurally identical: gain above unity, destabilization, crash.

### Feedback Systems ↔ Evolution
Natural selection is a feedback system without a designer: organisms that survive reproduce, passing on the traits that enabled survival, which shapes the distribution of traits in the next generation. The "setpoint" is whatever the environment rewards — and the environment itself changes in response to the organisms within it (coevolution). Feedback all the way down, and no one set the gain.

## Teaching Notes

### Common Student Insights
- "I always thought of feedback as something to avoid — distortion, noise, runaway systems. This week I'm thinking of it as a compositional material. The system's response to itself is its character."
- "The filter in the feedback path is the most important thing. I spent an hour changing only that and the track completely transformed. The law of the loop is the law of the system."
- "Building the visualizer made the abstract mathematical concept physical. I could see the amplitude growing when feedback was above 100%. I understood gain in a way I never had from just reading about it."

### Common Struggles
- **Confusing feedback with reverb:** Students often use "feedback" to mean "reverb tail" or "echo." The conceptual work of this theme is distinguishing these: reverb dissipates (gain < 1 at every reflection); musical feedback sustains or diverges (gain ≥ 1 in some path).
- **Managing the Track 3 setup safely:** Real acoustic feedback from a speaker-microphone loop can damage hearing if gain is set too high. Students need instruction in safe gain staging before attempting this track. The modular synthesis alternative is safer and equally instructive.
- **The nonlinear regime:** Students who push feedback above unity often panic at the divergence and pull back immediately. The assignment is to explore the edge — to understand what the nonlinear regime feels like, not to avoid it.

### The Breakthrough Moment
When a student realizes that the resonant filter in Track 2 is "playing" pitches — that the note they are hearing is not in the original signal but is generated by the system's self-amplification at a specific frequency — and that they can play these system-generated pitches as a melodic instrument.

## Extensions & Variations

### For Advanced Students
- Implement a digital feedback delay system from scratch in code (Python, Max/MSP, or SuperCollider). The implementation requires understanding the circular buffer — a data structure that is itself a feedback system (write pointer chasing read pointer). Write the code; hear how the implementation decisions shape the musical result.
- Study Alvin Lucier's *I Am Sitting in a Room* (1969): a piece built entirely on acoustic feedback — Lucier's voice recorded, played back into the room, re-recorded, repeated until the room's resonant frequencies dominate and the voice disappears. The room is the system; the resonant frequencies are the attractors.
- Explore chaotic feedback systems: the logistic map, iterated as a feedback equation, produces periodic, quasi-periodic, and chaotic behavior depending on the gain parameter. Map this mathematically and aurally.

### For Beginners
- Find a single delay pedal with a feedback knob. Set delay time to 500ms. Slowly turn the feedback from 0% to 100% while playing a sustained note. Listen to the evolution at every gain setting. You have now heard the full feedback spectrum from dissipation to sustained loop.
- Listen to Hendrix's *Machine Gun* (1970) — specifically the feedback held between phrases. He is not playing the guitar; he is playing the feedback system formed by guitar + amplifier + room position. The instrument is the loop.
- Ask yourself: is there any creative system you use that doesn't involve feedback? (There isn't. The question is whether the feedback is fast, slow, useful, or damaging.)
