# Interview Subjects — Book and Web Series

This document maps every Weekly Theme to the best living interview subjects. Where the theme's primary philosopher is still living, they're listed first as a **primary source**. Where the philosopher is historical, the list pivots to living practitioners, theorists, or artists who embody the theme most directly.

One note on method: the goal is not credential collection. The best interviews in this kind of book are with people who *practice* the ideas — who have built something, failed at something, or changed their mind about something. Prioritize the ones who will speak from experience, not just expertise.

---

## The One Living Primary Source in the Curriculum

### John Chowning — FM Synthesis (Week 36)
*Stanford University Center for Computer Research in Music and Acoustics (CCRMA)*

The only philosopher/primary source in the entire 40-week curriculum who is still living. Chowning discovered FM synthesis in 1967, published the paper in 1973, and licensed it to Yamaha — whose DX7 defined the sound of the 1980s. He is now in his early 90s and still periodically active at CCRMA.

**Why this interview is essential:** The theme's central claim — that extraordinary complexity emerges from the interaction of two simple things — comes directly from his discovery. He can describe what it felt like to hear a new spectrum appear from two sine waves, which is the most direct illustration of emergence the curriculum contains.

**Contact via:** Stanford CCRMA. He has given recent interviews and is accessible through the institution.

## The Foundational Weeks (Weeks 1–5)
*The Kick Drum Paradox, Four on the Floor, The 808, The First Sound, High Pass Low Pass*

These weeks don't have assigned philosophers — they're entry points through production fundamentals. The interview subjects here should be practitioners who can make the physics and history of these sounds feel alive.

**Arthur Baker** *(born 1955)*
Producer and remixer who shaped early electronic music and hip-hop production; could speak to the 808 as a compositional tool in its original context.

**Larry Heard (Mr. Fingers)** *(born ~1960)*
Chicago house pioneer. Can speak to the foundational role of the kick drum and bass relationship in the music that invented an entire sonic vocabulary — and to the emotional charge of the simplest rhythmic elements.

**Rick Rubin** *(born 1963)*
Can speak to the paradox of reduction: how stripping production to fundamentals (kick, bass, vocal) is itself a sophisticated decision. His career is a 40-year argument for the primacy of first sounds.

## Weeks 6–20: Fundamental Themes

### Week 6 — The Loop / Friedrich Nietzsche
**Steve Reich** *(born 1936)*
The composer who made the loop into a philosophical proposition — not just a structural convenience but an argument about time, repetition, and gradual change. *Music as a Gradual Process* is the essay version of what this week teaches. Now in his late 80s and still lucid about his intentions.

**Brian Eno** *(born 1948)*
Developed tape loop systems (with Robert Fripp) and ambient music as a genre built on looped repetition that changes through listening rather than composition. His *Oblique Strategies* cards are themselves a looping system for creativity.

---

### Week 7 — Reverb and Space / Martin Heidegger
**Barry Blesser** *(born ~1944)*
Author of *Spaces Speak, Are You Listening?* — the most important book on the cultural and psychological meanings of spatial acoustics. His concept of "aural architecture" is the practical equivalent of Heidegger's *Being-in-the-World* as applied to room sound.

**Bob Clearmountain** *(born 1956)*
The mixing engineer whose use of room and reverb has defined the spatial character of popular records for four decades. Can speak from practice rather than theory about what reverb communicates emotionally.

---

### Week 8 — The Sidechain Story / Martin Buber
**Andrew Scheps** *(born ~1970)*
Mixing engineer (Red Hot Chili Peppers, Adele, Jay-Z) who uses sidechain compression as a relational tool — shaping how elements listen to and respond to each other. Could speak directly to the I–Thou dynamic of the kick and bass relationship.

**Bob Katz** *(born ~1951)*
Mastering engineer and author of *Mastering Audio*, the most comprehensive technical and philosophical treatment of dynamics processing. Can frame sidechain compression within a broader argument about loudness, dynamics, and what recordings owe their listeners.

---

### Week 9 — Saturation and Warmth / Rumi
**Dave Hill** *(born ~1955)*
Founder of Crane Song Audio; designs analog and analog-modeling equipment specifically for the quality of saturation and harmonic coloration. Can explain what "warmth" actually is at the circuit level, and why it matters.

**Dave Derr**
Inventor of the Empirical Labs Distressor — the most widely used analog saturation/compression unit in contemporary recording. Has spoken thoughtfully about the relationship between analog imperfection and emotional resonance.

---

### Week 10 — The Breakdown / Aristotle
**Simon Reynolds** *(born 1963)*
British music critic and theorist, author of *Energy Flash* (published in the US as *Generation Ecstasy*) — the definitive cultural history of electronic dance music. Can frame the breakdown as a rhetorical device with a history, a politics, and a phenomenology.

**Larry Heard (Mr. Fingers)** *(see above — applicable here too)*
The breakdown in house music is partly his invention. He can speak to the emotional architecture of tension and release from the inside.

---

### Week 11 — The Bass Question / Pythagoras
**Victor Wooten** *(born 1964)*
Bass player, educator, and author of *The Music Lesson*. Thinks philosophically about bass — its role in music as foundation, its relationship to other voices, its cultural and psychoacoustic weight. His pedagogical work runs parallel to what this week teaches.

**Kyle Gann** *(born 1955)*
Composer and music theorist with extensive writing on just intonation and the overtone series. Can speak to why the bass note determines harmonic meaning — the Pythagorean claim — with both historical depth and compositional precision.

---

### Week 12 — The Grid and the Groove / Henri Bergson
**Vijay Iyer** *(born 1971)*
Jazz musician and Harvard professor whose doctoral work (at UC Berkeley with David Wessel) was specifically on rhythm, embodiment, and musical meaning. Has written and spoken extensively about the phenomenology of groove — duration as lived experience rather than measured time. The Bergson connection is explicit in his work.

**Anne Danielsen** *(born ~1965)*
Norwegian musicologist at the University of Oslo who has done the most rigorous empirical work on groove — measuring micro-timing deviations in studio recordings and analyzing what makes them feel right. Author of *Presence and Pleasure: The Funk Grooves of James Brown and Parliament*.

---

### Week 13 — Panning and Perspective / Maurice Merleau-Ponty
**Edgar Choueiri** *(born ~1963)*
Princeton professor of Applied Physics who has developed 3D audio reproduction systems (BACCH spatial audio). His work is explicitly about making the listening body the center of acoustic space — an applied Merleau-Ponty.

**Nina Sun Eidsheim** *(born ~1970s)*
UCLA musicologist whose *Sensing Sound: Singing and Listening as Vibrational Practice* is the most direct scholarly translation of Merleau-Ponty's embodied perception into music theory. Hearing is for her — as for Merleau-Ponty — always already a bodily act.

---

### Week 14 — The Voice / Emmanuel Levinas
**Adriana Cavarero** *(born 1947)*
Italian philosopher who wrote *For More Than One Voice* — a direct argument that philosophy has always subordinated the voice (the embodied, singular sound of a person speaking) to its content (logos). She is the living philosopher whose work most directly extends Levinas's ethics into the domain of voice and sound. This interview is essential.

**Nina Sun Eidsheim** *(see above — equally relevant here)*
Her book *The Race of Sound* is about how voice is racially categorized in ways that reveal the embodied, political dimensions of listening — exactly the ethical territory Levinas opens.

---

### Week 15 — The Sample / Walter Benjamin
**Paul D. Miller (DJ Spooky)** *(born 1970)*
Artist, musician, and theorist whose *Rhythm Science* (2004) is the most sustained philosophical treatment of sampling as a practice of memory, history, and cultural recombination. Has explicitly engaged with Benjamin's "The Work of Art in the Age of Mechanical Reproduction" in his theoretical work. An ideal interview.

**Joseph Schloss** *(born ~1969)*
Ethnomusicologist, author of *Making Beats: The Art of Sample-Based Hip-Hop*. Can speak to sampling from inside the practice — what producers think they're doing when they sample, what the ethics look like from within the community.

**DJ Premier** *(born 1966)*
One of the greatest sample-based producers in hip-hop history. Approaches sampling not as quotation but as transformation — the Benjamin question from inside the studio.

---

### Week 16 — Silence and Rest / John Cage
**Christian Wolff** *(born 1934)*
Composer who studied with Cage and was one of the original New York School composers (with Feldman and Brown). Now in his early 90s. Can speak to what Cage actually meant about silence from personal experience, and to the difference between Cage's philosophical position and his own.

**George Lewis** *(born 1952)*
Composer, trombonist, scholar. Author of *A Power Stronger Than Itself: The AACM and American Experimental Music*. Can speak to how experimental music traditions outside the Cage lineage — specifically Black experimental music — have used silence differently. Essential counterweight to the Cage-centric framing.

---

### Week 17 — Arrangement as Architecture / Christopher Alexander
Christopher Alexander died in 2022. His ideas live on most directly through:

**Steven Johnson** *(born 1968)*
Author of *Where Good Ideas Come From* and *Emergence*. Thinks about structure, pattern, and the conditions for creativity in ways that parallel Alexander's pattern language. Can frame arrangement as a generative constraint system.

**Brian Eno** *(see above — applicable here too)*
His concept of "scenius" (genius as environmental and collaborative, not individual) and his studio practice as environmental design are Alexandrian in spirit.

---

### Week 18 — The Transition / Victor Turner
**Simon Reynolds** *(see above)*
Has written extensively about the rave as a liminal space — Turner's threshold experience enacted in a warehouse or field. His cultural history of electronic music is implicitly an analysis of manufactured liminality.

**Bill Brewster and Frank Broughton**
Co-authors of *Last Night a DJ Saved My Life* — the history of the DJ. Can speak to the transition as the DJ's primary act of meaning-making: two records, one moment, the listener held between worlds.

---

### Week 19 — The Accident / Karl Popper
**Brian Eno** *(see above — essential here)*
The *Oblique Strategies* cards were designed specifically to introduce productive accidents into the creative process. His entire aesthetic philosophy is organized around creating conditions in which accidents are more likely and more useful than intentions. This interview is central to this week.

**David Eagleman** *(born 1971)*
Neuroscientist and author, has written about the role of constraints and errors in creativity and learning. Can speak to the cognitive science behind why accidents produce insight.

---

### Week 20 — The Reference Track / Thomas Kuhn
**Bob Katz** *(see above)*
His work on loudness normalization and the history of the "loudness wars" is a case study in paradigm shift — an industry-wide reference standard that shifted, and the chaos and resolution that followed.

**Simon Reynolds** *(see above)*
His concept of "retromania" (from the 2011 book) is directly a Kuhnian analysis: the current historical moment as one in which popular music cycles through previous paradigms rather than generating new ones. Can speak to what it means when a genre *has* a reference track rather than discovering one.

---

## Weeks 21–40: Deep Themes

### Week 21 — Harmonic Series and Natural Resonance / Pythagoras
**Marc Sabat** *(born 1965)*
Canadian composer based in Berlin, works entirely in just intonation derived from the harmonic series. Co-developer of the Extended Helmholtz-Ellis JI Pitch Notation system. Can speak to the practical and philosophical experience of building music from harmonic series ratios as a daily compositional act.

**Kyle Gann** *(born 1955)*
Has written more extensively and accessibly about just intonation and the harmonic series than almost any living writer. His online writings on microtonality and just intonation are a primary resource for the field.

---

### Week 22 — Klangfarbenmelodie / Arnold Schoenberg
**Tristan Murail** *(born 1947)*
Co-founder (with Gérard Grisey) of spectral music — the movement that directly built what Schoenberg described. Now teaching at Columbia University. Can speak to why spectral music is the direct realization of Klangfarbenmelodie, what the technology (sonogram analysis) made possible, and what composing in timbral space actually feels like.

**Kaija Saariaho** passed in 2023. **George Benjamin** *(born 1960)* studied with Messiaen and Grisey and is the most prominent British composer working in the spectral tradition. Can speak to how timbral composition shapes emotional and formal structure.

---

### Week 23 — Gamelan and Emergent Complexity / John Holland
**Evan Ziporyn** *(born 1959)*
MIT composer and clarinetist who has spent decades studying and performing Balinese gamelan. Can speak to how gamelan's emergent complexity is experienced from inside the ensemble — not as a systemic phenomenon you analyze but as a social and musical reality you live inside.

**Melanie Mitchell** *(born ~1969)*
Computer scientist and complexity theorist at the Santa Fe Institute. Author of *Complexity: A Guided Tour*. Can frame John Holland's legacy and make the connection between genetic algorithms, emergence, and musical systems explicit.

**Steven Johnson** *(see above)*
His *Emergence* book covers gamelan directly as a case of emergent complexity. Can speak to what the gamelan tells us about how complex order arises from simple local interactions.

---

### Week 24 — Spectral Composition / Gérard Grisey
**Tristan Murail** *(see above — primary interview for this week)*

**Joshua Fineberg** *(born 1969)*
Composer and theorist at Boston University who studied with Murail and has written extensively on spectral music. Author of *Classical Music: Why Bother?* Can explain the spectral method analytically and speak to what it produces emotionally.

---

### Week 25 — Microtonality / Harry Partch
**Georg Friedrich Haas** *(born 1953)*
Austrian composer whose microtonal language is among the most distinctive in contemporary classical music. Works extensively in spectral and just intonation systems. Can speak to what it costs and what it opens to build a compositional practice outside 12-TET.

**Kyle Gann** *(see above — essential here)*

**La Monte Young** *(born 1935)*
Founder of minimalism and the most radical practitioner of just intonation in American music. His *The Well-Tuned Piano* is the supreme example of a musical practice derived entirely from the harmonic series. Now in his early 90s. The interview requires significant effort to arrange but would be irreplaceable.

**Marc Sabat** *(see above)*

---

### Week 26 — Metric Modulation / Gilles Deleuze
**Vijay Iyer** *(see above — essential here)*
Has explicitly engaged with Deleuzian concepts of duration and difference in his scholarly writing about rhythm and improvisation. The Deleuze connection is not a stretch — it is part of his theoretical vocabulary. This is the best available interview for this week.

**Justin London** *(born ~1960)*
Music theorist at Carleton College, author of *Hearing in Time: Psychological Aspects of Musical Meter*. Can speak to the cognitive science of how we perceive and process metric change — the empirical ground beneath the philosophical claim.

---

### Week 27 — Feedback Systems / Norbert Wiener
**Robert Fripp** *(born 1946)*
Guitarist who developed "Frippertronics" — a tape loop feedback system that produces sustained, evolving drones from guitar input. Has practiced and theorized feedback as a compositional principle for 50 years. Can speak to feedback not as an accident but as an instrument.

**Donella Meadows** died in 2001. Her *Thinking in Systems* is now taught by others; the best available proxy is:

**Daniel Schmachtenberger** *(born ~1980)*
Systems theorist who has spoken extensively about feedback dynamics in complex systems — social, ecological, and political. Could frame the Wienerian legacy in contemporary terms.

**Lee Ranaldo** *(born 1956)*
Member of Sonic Youth who has used guitar feedback as primary melodic material throughout his career. Can speak to feedback as composition from inside a practice that made it iconic.

---

### Week 28 — Convolution / Charles Sanders Peirce
**Angelo Farina** *(born ~1960)*
Italian acoustic engineer at the University of Parma who has done foundational work on impulse response measurement and convolution reverb. The person most responsible for making acoustic fingerprinting (capturing the reverberation signature of specific spaces) a practical compositional tool.

**Chris Kyriakakis**
USC professor and spatial audio researcher; can speak to what convolution with real acoustic spaces reveals about the relationship between sound and place.

---

### Week 29 — The Shepard Tone / M.C. Escher
**Roger Shepard** *(born 1929)*
The cognitive psychologist who invented the Shepard tone. Now in his late 90s at Stanford. The interview would be brief but the proximity to the original discovery is irreplaceable — he can describe what he was trying to demonstrate about auditory perception and what surprised him about how the illusion worked.

**Diana Deutsch** *(born 1938)*
Professor of psychology at UC San Diego, the foremost researcher in auditory illusions. Her *Musical Illusions and Paradoxes* recordings (1995) document the full range of perceptual phenomena related to the Shepard tone. Can speak to what these illusions reveal about how the brain constructs musical experience from ambiguous acoustic data. An essential interview.

---

### Week 30 — Phase Cancellation / Niels Bohr
**Bob Katz** *(see above)*
Has written and spoken more clearly about phase in the context of practical audio than almost anyone. Can frame phase cancellation as a fact engineers work with daily and a concept that reveals the wave nature of sound.

**Bob Clearmountain** *(see above)*
The mixing engineer's relationship to phase is entirely practical — you either hear it or you don't, and the consequences are immediate. Can speak to phase from inside the experience of problem-solving under deadline.

---

### Week 31 — Granular Synthesis / Heraclitus
**Curtis Roads** *(born 1951)*
Author of *Microsound* (MIT Press, 2001) — the definitive text on granular synthesis and its historical, technical, and aesthetic dimensions. Also wrote *The Computer Music Tutorial*. Has been working in granular synthesis since the 1970s. The primary living scholar and practitioner of this technique. This interview is essential.

**Barry Truax** *(born 1947)*
Canadian composer who developed the first real-time granular synthesis systems in the 1980s. His *Handbook for Acoustic Ecology* and his Soundscape research at Simon Fraser University connect granular synthesis to acoustic ecology — the idea that sound environments have meaning and that granular synthesis can capture and transform the grain of real acoustic life.

---

### Week 32 — The Haas Effect / Immanuel Kant
**Edgar Choueiri** *(see above)*
His 3D audio research depends on the precise manipulation of inter-aural time differences — the mechanism the Haas effect exploits. Can explain what the 35ms window reveals about how the auditory system constructs spatial experience from time differences.

**Diana Deutsch** *(see above)*
The Haas effect is adjacent to her broader research on auditory illusion and spatial perception. Can frame it within the larger picture of how the brain resolves ambiguous spatial information.

---

### Week 33 — The Harmonic Oscillator / Isaac Newton
**Tom Oberheim** *(born 1936)*
Pioneer synthesizer designer (the Oberheim Four-Voice, the OB-X) who built oscillator systems that have shaped the sound of popular music for 50 years. Now in his late 80s. Can speak to the oscillator not as an abstract physical system but as a specific design decision with musical consequences.

**Julius O. Smith** *(born ~1952)*
Stanford professor at CCRMA, expert in digital signal processing and synthesis. His online textbooks on physical modeling and signal processing are foundational resources. Can speak to the oscillator at every level from physics to musical result.

---

### Week 34 — The Overtone Series / Jean-Philippe Rameau
**La Monte Young** *(see above)*
His entire musical practice is built on the direct perceptual experience of the overtone series. Has spoken and written about what happens when you sustain pure intervals long enough for the acoustic physics to become audible — when the mathematics and the experience collapse into each other.

**Tanya Tagaq** *(born 1975)*
Inuit throat singer and experimental musician. Can speak to a vocal practice built entirely on harmonic overtones — a living tradition in which the overtone series is not a theoretical construct but a performative technique. Brings a completely different cultural entry point to the same acoustic phenomenon.

**Marc Sabat** *(see above)*

---

### Week 35 — Additive Synthesis / Joseph Fourier
**Julius O. Smith** *(see above)*
Can speak to Fourier analysis and additive synthesis at every level — the mathematics, the signal processing implementation, and the musical application. His *Spectral Audio Signal Processing* is the most comprehensive available text.

**John Chowning** *(see above)*
Before FM, he worked with additive synthesis at CCRMA. Can speak to the transition from additive (building from components) to FM (generating complexity from interaction) as a compositional and philosophical choice.

---

### Week 37 — Physical Modeling / Ludwig Wittgenstein
**Perry Cook** *(born ~1964)*
Co-developer (with Julius Smith) of the Synthesis ToolKit (STK) and author of *Physical Audio Signal Processing*. Can speak to physical modeling from inside its development — what the technique reveals about the relationship between acoustic physics and musical expression, and why modeling a violin is not the same as playing one.

**Julius O. Smith** *(see above — foundational for this week too)*

---

### Week 38 — The Transfer Function / Claude Shannon
**Sergio Verdú** *(born 1958)*
Princeton information theorist who has extended Shannon's work on channel capacity and information transmission. Can speak to what Shannon's theory actually says about musical communication — what is preserved and what is lost in transmission.

**Julius O. Smith** *(see above)*
Can speak to the transfer function as an engineering concept with direct musical application — how every element of a signal chain (microphone, preamp, speaker, room) is a transfer function that shapes what passes through it.

---

### Week 39 — Stochastic Synthesis / Iannis Xenakis
**Curtis Roads** *(see above)*
Wrote extensively about Xenakis and stochastic synthesis in *The Computer Music Tutorial* and *Microsound*. Can speak to Xenakis's mathematical compositional systems as both a historical achievement and a living influence.

**George Lewis** *(see above)*
His work with AACM and with AI improvisation systems involves stochastic and probabilistic processes. Can speak to randomness as a compositional strategy from a perspective completely different from Xenakis's — improvisational and collaborative rather than compositionally deterministic.

**Clarence Barlow** *(born 1945)*
German-Indian composer who has worked with algorithmic and stochastic composition since the 1970s. His concept of "harmonicity" and his stochastic compositional tools are direct descendants of the Xenakis tradition.

---

### Week 40 — The Impulse Response / Hermann von Helmholtz
**Angelo Farina** *(see above — primary interview for this week)*
His work on impulse response measurement and auralization (the acoustic reconstruction of physical spaces) is the direct practical realization of Helmholtz's resonance theory. Can speak to what it means to capture the acoustic fingerprint of a space and use it compositionally.

**Barry Truax** *(see above)*
His soundscape research uses impulse responses and acoustic analysis as tools for understanding the meaning of specific sonic environments. Can connect the technical concept to questions of place, identity, and ecological listening.

## Cross-Cutting Subjects

These people span multiple themes and would be among the most valuable interviews for the overall arc of the book and series.

**Brian Eno** *(born 1948)*
Relevant to: The Loop, The Accident, Arrangement as Architecture, Silence, and arguably most of the philosophy section. His thinking about music as process, ambient as environmental design, and the studio as compositional instrument cuts across more themes than any other single figure. An essential long interview.

**Curtis Roads** *(born 1951)*
Relevant to: Granular Synthesis, Stochastic Synthesis, Additive Synthesis, The Impulse Response. Has written the most important technical/aesthetic books in computer music. A single long interview would cover four weeks.

**Kyle Gann** *(born 1955)*
Relevant to: Microtonality, The Harmonic Series, The Overtone Series, The Bass Question. Has written more accessibly and rigorously about just intonation and American experimental music than anyone else alive.

**Vijay Iyer** *(born 1971)*
Relevant to: The Grid and the Groove, Metric Modulation, The Bass Question, and the philosophy of improvisation throughout. Unique because he is both a practicing musician and a rigorous academic thinker — he can speak to the ideas from inside the practice.

**Julius O. Smith** *(born ~1952)*
Relevant to: Physical Modeling, Additive Synthesis, The Harmonic Oscillator, The Transfer Function. The most important living technical figure in digital synthesis and signal processing.

**Simon Reynolds** *(born 1963)*
Relevant to: The Loop, The Breakdown, The Reference Track, The Sample, The Transition. Has written the most culturally and philosophically rich history of electronic music. Can frame many of the themes within a broader argument about music, time, and culture.

**Bob Katz** *(born ~1951)*
Relevant to: Saturation and Warmth, The Sidechain Story, Phase Cancellation, The Reference Track. Bridges technical precision and aesthetic philosophy in a way that is rare among practitioners.

**Diana Deutsch** *(born 1938)*
Relevant to: The Shepard Tone, The Haas Effect, Panning and Perspective, The Harmonic Series. The foremost living researcher on auditory illusion and musical perception. Can ground multiple themes in perceptual science.

**Nina Sun Eidsheim** *(born ~1970s)*
Relevant to: The Voice, Panning and Perspective, Klangfarbenmelodie. The scholar who has done the most rigorous and politically engaged work on embodied listening and vocal identity.

**Marc Sabat** *(born 1965)*
Relevant to: Microtonality, The Harmonic Series, The Overtone Series, Klangfarbenmelodie. Working composer in just intonation who can speak from daily practice.

## Artists from the Listening Examples

Every theme includes a *Music to Study* list. Many of those artists are living and would make excellent interview subjects — not as scholars of the theme but as practitioners who have embodied it. These interviews would work differently from the philosopher-proxy interviews above: less "explain the concept" and more "describe the decision, the accident, the failure, the thing you noticed."

Dead artists from the listening lists (not listed here): Alvin Lucier (2021), Jimi Hendrix (1970), J Dilla (2006), Morton Feldman (1987), Fela Kuti (1997), Marvin Gaye (1984), Jeff Buckley (1997), Kaija Saariaho (2023), Ravi Shankar (2012), Jean-Claude Risset (2016), Ryuichi Sakamoto (2023). Their work is referenced throughout the themes; their interviews must be found in archives.

---

### Electronic Music Producers and Composers

**Brian Eno** *(born 1948)* — Weeks 6, 7, 16, 17, 19, 28
The most-referenced living artist in the curriculum. Appears in the listening lists for Silence and Rest (*Thursday Afternoon*), Reverb and Space (*Ambient 4: On Land*), Convolution, Arrangement as Architecture (*Selected Ambient Works Vol. II* via ambient analogy), and The Accident. Already listed as a philosophy-proxy subject; his interviews as a practitioner are equally essential.

**Aphex Twin (Richard D. James)** *(born 1971)* — Weeks 19, 22, 31, 36
Appears in the listening lists for The Accident (*Drukqs*), Klangfarbenmelodie (*Selected Ambient Works Vol. II*), Granular Synthesis (*Selected Ambient Works Vol. II*), and FM Synthesis. A central figure in multiple deep themes — his work explores FM synthesis, granular texture, constrained randomness, and timbral composition simultaneously. Famously difficult to reach but has given extended interviews (particularly with publications like The Wire and Pitchfork) when he chooses to. An interview on any of these themes would be valuable.

**Autechre (Sean Booth and Rob Brown)** *(born ~1971)* — Weeks 15 (High Pass Low Pass), 19, 37
Referenced for high-pass/low-pass filtering (*Gantz Graf*), constrained randomness as compositional philosophy (*LP5*), and physical modeling-adjacent electronic percussion (*Tri Repetae*). Their interviews are infrequent but substantive — they speak carefully about their process, constraint systems, and the relationship between rule and result.

**Burial (William Bevan)** *(born ~1979)* — Weeks 7, 17
Referenced for Reverb and Space and Arrangement as Architecture (*Untrue*). Notoriously anonymous and has given almost no interviews. Worth attempting; the reverb in his work is a philosophical position about memory and place, not just an aesthetic choice.

**Four Tet (Kieran Hebden)** *(born 1977)* — Week 18
Referenced for The Transition (*There Is Love in You*). Has given extensive interviews about his production approach and the craft of seamless emotional movement in electronic music. Accessible and articulate.

**Jon Hopkins** *(born 1979)* — Week 17
Referenced for Arrangement as Architecture (*Immunity*). Has spoken thoughtfully about the album as a complete structural gesture — the production philosophy behind an architecture that sustains across an hour. An accessible interview.

**Fennesz (Christian Fennesz)** *(born 1962)* — Week 27
Referenced for Feedback Systems (*Endless Summer*). Austrian guitarist and electronic musician who uses laptop feedback loops as harmonic texture. Can speak to feedback as composition from inside a practice that made it central rather than incidental.

**William Basinski** *(born 1958)* — Weeks 7, 31
Referenced for Reverb and Space and Granular Synthesis (*Disintegration Loops*). The *Disintegration Loops* (created when aging tape deteriorated during digitization on September 11, 2001) are the most famous accidental granular composition in recent memory. Can speak to both Reverb as memory and the accident as compositional event.

**Plastikman (Richie Hawtin)** *(born 1970)* — Week 6 (Four on the Floor)
Referenced for repetition as hypnosis (*Spastik*). One of the most thoughtful practitioners of minimal techno, has given substantive interviews about the relationship between repetition, time perception, and trance states.

**Arca (Alejandra Ghersi)** *(born 1989)* — Weeks 31, 36
Referenced for Granular Synthesis and FM Synthesis (*Mutant*). Works extensively with FM synthesis pushed into inharmonic, metallic territory, and with granular texture as primary compositional material. A distinctive voice on what synthesis feels like from inside a practice that treats it as raw material for emotional extremity.

**Flying Lotus (Steven Ellison)** *(born 1983)* — Weeks 11, 12
Referenced for The Bass Question (*Cosmogramma*) and The Grid and the Groove (*Cosmogramma*). Can speak to bass as an orchestral element and to groove as something that resists quantization while still feeling locked. His work inherits J Dilla's micro-timing philosophy directly.

**Grouper (Liz Harris)** *(born ~1980)* — Week 31
Referenced for Granular Synthesis (*Dragging a Dead Deer Up a Hill*). Uses granular processing of voice as environmental field — the voice dissolved into texture. Can speak to granular synthesis not as a technical tool but as an expressive condition.

**Madlib** *(born 1973)* — Week 15
Referenced for The Sample (*Shades of Blue*). One of the most respected sample-based producers, can speak to sampling as both archival practice and active transformation — the source material as both quotation and raw material.

**DJ Shadow (Josh Davis)** *(born 1972)* — Week 15
Referenced for The Sample (*Endtroducing...*). Made the first album composed entirely from samples. Has spoken extensively about the practice of crate digging, the ethics of sampling, and what it means to build a new statement entirely from prior statements.

**Alva Noto (Carsten Nicolai)** *(born 1965)* — Weeks 5 (High Pass Low Pass), 1 (The First Sound)
Referenced for high-pass/low-pass filtering (*Xerrox* series) and for the first sound as generative process. German artist who works at the intersection of visual art and electronic music; can speak to filtering as compositional language and to how a first sound determines everything that follows.

**Deadmau5** *(born 1981)* — Week 10
Referenced for The Breakdown (*Strobe*). Unusually patient among EDM producers — has spoken publicly about the philosophical choice to make a breakdown that outlasts most full tracks. Can speak to tension-and-release as a structural argument rather than a convention.

**Amon Tobin** *(born 1972)* — Week 19
Referenced for The Accident (*ISAM*). Built an album around sounds with "organic behavioral logic" — deliberate accidents designed to feel found rather than constructed. Can speak to what it means to design an accident.

### Rock, Pop, and Singer-Songwriter Artists

**Björk** *(born 1965)* — Week 14
Referenced for The Voice (*Homogenic*). Treats the voice as an orchestral instrument while preserving its grain. Has given some of the most thoughtful interviews about voice, body, and production of any contemporary artist. An essential interview if accessible.

**Thom Yorke / Nigel Godrich** *(Radiohead/Yorke solo, both born ~1968/1971)* — Week 13
Referenced for Panning and Perspective (*OK Computer* — Godrich's spatial decisions). Nigel Godrich is the more likely interview subject on the specifics of stereo and spatial design. Can speak to how spatial placement functions as emotional weather rather than just balance.

**FKA twigs** *(born 1988)* — Week 14
Referenced for The Voice (*Magdalene*). Uses the voice as a body undergoing transformation — the grain survives intensive processing. Can speak to what it means to keep the vocal body present inside radical production.

**James Blake** *(born 1988)* — Weeks 11, 5
Referenced for The Bass Question and The Kick Drum Paradox. Can speak to sub-bass as primary melodic and emotional voice — has built a practice around the idea that the low end can carry lyric content.

**Bon Iver (Justin Vernon)** *(born 1981)* — Week 14
Referenced for The Voice (*For Emma, Forever Ago*). Made a record about voice as confession, then dissolved the voice into texture on subsequent records. Can speak to the grain of the voice as both intimate presence and compositional material.

**Billie Eilish** *(born 2001)* — Week 14
Referenced for The Voice. Her use of proximity effect and whisper as compositional philosophy makes her one of the most distinctive vocal practitioners of her generation. At 24 in 2026, a different kind of interview — but her awareness of what she's doing with close-mic technique is genuine and well-documented.

**Max Richter** *(born 1966)* — Week 1 (The First Sound)
Referenced for The First Sound (*On the Nature of Daylight*). A composer who thinks carefully about what it means for a piece of music to begin — how the opening establishes its entire emotional contract. Has given thoughtful interviews about structure and emotional intent.

### Classical and Experimental Composers

**Steve Reich** *(born 1936)* — Weeks 6, 26
Referenced for The Loop (*Music for 18 Musicians*) and Metric Modulation (*Drumming*). Already listed as a philosophy-proxy interview. His *Music as a Gradual Process* essay is the direct theoretical statement of what The Loop week teaches; an interview would cover both.

**Terry Riley** *(born 1935)* — Week 6
Referenced for The Loop (*In C*). The founder of minimalism as a compositional system built on loop and gradual process. Now in his early 90s. Speaks with great warmth and clarity about the origins of *In C* and what he was trying to discover about repetition.

**Tristan Murail** *(born 1947)* — Weeks 21, 22, 24
Referenced in the listening lists for Harmonic Series, Klangfarbenmelodie, and Spectral Composition (*Gondwana*). Already listed as a primary philosophy-proxy. The listening list appearances confirm: this is an essential interview.

**Arvo Pärt** *(born 1935)* — Week 16
Referenced for Silence and Rest (*Spiegel im Spiegel*). The composer who formalized tintinnabuli — a practice of silence and restraint in which what is absent carries as much weight as what sounds. Now in his early 90s, still living in Berlin. An interview on silence would be unlike any other.

**Ralf Hütter (Kraftwerk)** *(born 1946)* — Week 1 (The Kick Drum Paradox)
Referenced for *Numbers* — the kick as timekeeper in minimal electronic context. Florian Schneider died in 2020. Ralf Hütter rarely gives interviews but has spoken about Kraftwerk's philosophy of reduction and the machine as the primary voice. An interview would be rare and significant.

**Axel Dörner** *(born ~1964)* — Week 35 (Additive Synthesis)
Referenced for solo trumpet work that reveals the additive structure of a brass instrument through spectral playing. A Berlin-based improviser who has collaborated extensively in the European free improvisation scene; can speak to the overtone series as a physical and compositional reality inside acoustic instrument technique.

### Producers and Engineers (From Listening Contexts)

**Geoff Emerick** passed in 2018. His work on *Abbey Road* (Week 13) is best discussed by those who knew him or study his technique.

**Thomas Bangalter (Daft Punk)** *(born 1975)* — Weeks 3, 8
Referenced for The 808 and The Sidechain Story. Daft Punk disbanded in 2021 but Bangalter has returned to composing (his ballet score *Mythologies*). Can speak to sidechain compression as a compositional choice — "One More Time" is the textbook example — from inside a production practice that made it iconic.

**Chemical Brothers (Tom Rowlands and Ed Simons)** *(born ~1970/1971)* — Week 10
Referenced for The Breakdown (*Block Rockin' Beats*). Have given occasional in-depth interviews about the maximal approach to the drop and breakdown — the shock value as a formal argument.

**Underworld (Karl Hyde)** *(born 1957)* — Week 10
Referenced for The Breakdown (*Born Slippy .NUXX*). Karl Hyde has spoken in interviews and in his book *Teatime for Dreamers* about the narrative and emotional architecture of Underworld's productions. Can speak to the breakdown as a sustained ecstatic gesture.

## Additional Cross-Cutting Notes for the Series

Several artists span multiple themes in ways that make them excellent long-form interview subjects — a single conversation could anchor multiple episodes:

- **Brian Eno**: Loop → Silence → Accident → Arrangement → Convolution → Reverb → Feedback (7 themes)
- **Aphex Twin**: Accident → FM Synthesis → Granular Synthesis → Klangfarbenmelodie (4 themes)
- **Autechre**: Filtering → Accident → Physical Modeling (3 themes)
- **Steve Reich**: Loop → Metric Modulation (2 themes, foundational to both)
- **William Basinski**: Reverb and Space → Granular Synthesis (2 themes with a single conceptual arc about decay and memory)
- **Questlove**: Saturation → The Grid and the Groove (2 themes with a shared argument about the feel of recorded music)
- **Tristan Murail**: Harmonic Series → Klangfarbenmelodie → Spectral Composition (3 themes as a single compositional philosophy)
